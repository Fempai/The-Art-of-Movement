# PRO701 — Professional Repertory Laboratory

**Advanced conservatory seminar | Four 60-minute instructional lectures | Original course manuscript**

**Scope:** Polish, rehearsal, interpretation, stamina. **Expected learning:** Sustain technically controlled performance. These lectures contain timed instruction, interpretive frameworks, structured studio tasks, reading prompts, and assessed assignments. This is an independent nonaccredited curriculum. The lecture plans are not a substitute for supervised advanced performance training.

## Lecture 1: Polish as repeatable clarity

### Essential question
Which quality distinguishes a deliberate artistic choice from an accident?

### 0–15 minutes — Conceptual lecture
Professional polish is not maximal range or relentless intensity. It includes clean initiations, legible transitions, rhythm, placement, performance intention, and reliable recovery. A polished phrase should be repeatable at different rehearsal stages without accumulating unsafe compensation. Distinguish an intentional rough texture from uncontrolled execution.

The central methodological challenge is to distinguish **observation**, **interpretation**, and **evaluation**. Observation describes events that can be checked against a movement score or performance. Interpretation explains possible significance in light of form, history, and context. Evaluation assesses a claim against criteria that must be stated. In the conservatory, the student should articulate each stage rather than treating a personal response as universal evidence. This discipline supports artistic freedom: the goal is not to prescribe one aesthetic, but to make artistic choices more precise and discussable.

### 15–30 minutes — Analytical demonstration
**Instructional focus:** Begin with the relevant foundational study note and a scholarly source listed in the bibliography; identify author, context, and limitations. Timed phrase rehearsal and deliberate recovery

Analyze the task along four dimensions: body action, spatial pathway, temporal organization, and expressive quality. Which dimension remains stable? Which changes? Consider at least two alternatives and predict their consequences. Make predictions before rehearsing, then distinguish outcomes from expectations. Where physical risk or consent is relevant, use a low-intensity adaptation or a written staging simulation rather than unsafe experimentation.

### 30–45 minutes — Studio laboratory
Perform three low-intensity runs and score transitions, timing, intention and fatigue response independently.

**Movement material:** Counts 1–2 establish stance; 3–4 execute a comfortable version of the focus action; 5–6 repeat with a directional change; 7–8 recover and suspend.

**Creative transfer:** Apply polish to Undercurrent's reach-and-gather motif. Document counts, direction, musical accent, and artistic intention.

Use the original *Undercurrent* motif—step and settle (1–2), gather (3–4), diagonal reach (5–6), suspend (7–8)—as a controlled reference phrase. Preserve its identity in the first version, alter one formal parameter in the second, and annotate what changed. At advanced levels, artistic complexity is not measured by how extreme a body position becomes.

### 45–55 minutes — Research seminar
Consult at least one source from the bibliography. Identify the author's question, method or critical framework, historical/cultural position, and limits of applicability. Ask whether a cited claim is empirical, interpretive, or professional guidance. Avoid treating a textbook's descriptive vocabulary as proof of a universal psychological response.

**Discussion:** Which quality distinguishes a deliberate artistic choice from an accident? Explain two plausible positions and the evidence each would need. Describe one observation that would change your initial interpretation.

### 55–60 minutes — Retrieval and assessment
**Immediate recall:** Without consulting notes, define the central concept, apply it to the eight-count motif, and identify a limitation or alternative interpretation.

**Assignment:** Write 150–250 words answering: Which quality distinguishes a deliberate artistic choice from an accident? Include an observation, a technical explanation, and one revision to the phrase.

**Evidence of learning:** Academic: correct terminology and source awareness. Technical: pain-free control, consistent rhythm and repeatable transitions. Creative: intentional and legible variation. No pass/fail on extreme range. Submit a short annotated movement score or staging plan, a written argument (300–500 words), and a revision memo. Academic rubric: precision 30%, reasoning and evidence 30%, contextual ethics 20%, clarity and revision 20%. Performance technique is evaluated separately by a qualified instructor where applicable. This rubric has not been externally psychometrically validated.

## Lecture 2: Rehearsal architecture

### Essential question
How would you teach this to a dancer using words and a floor diagram?

### 0–15 minutes — Conceptual lecture
A rehearsal plan should separate warm-up, targeted correction, phrase drilling, run-through, and debrief. Error clustering helps prioritize recurring transition problems. Whole-piece repetition alone may reinforce errors when the dancer is fatigued. Design a rehearsal that alternates focused problem solving with integrated practice.

## Lecture 3: Interpretation and artistic negotiation

What observation would demonstrate improvement?

### 0–15 minutes — Conceptual lecture
Repertory interpretation balances the choreographer's formal constraints with the performer's embodied understanding. Communicate what must remain fixed—pathway, counts, spacing, safety—and what can vary—effort, focus, emotional texture. Rehearsal feedback should describe observable choices rather than demand that performers manufacture an emotion.

## Lecture 4: Stamina, pacing and recovery

### Essential question
Which quality distinguishes a deliberate artistic choice from an accident?

### 0–15 minutes — Conceptual lecture
Performance stamina includes aerobic and anaerobic demands, attention, and neuromuscular consistency. Repeated full-out runs without rest can degrade quality. Use planned intervals and monitor breath, timing, and coordination. Recovery is part of the rehearsal design rather than a sign of insufficient commitment.
