# PRO702 — Stagecraft & Artistic Direction

**Advanced conservatory seminar | Four 60-minute instructional lectures | Original course manuscript**

**Scope:** Lighting, sightlines, costume safety, rehearsal leadership. **Expected learning:** Present an intentional staging plan. These lectures contain timed instruction, interpretive frameworks, structured studio tasks, reading prompts, and assessed assignments. This is an independent nonaccredited curriculum. The lecture plans are not a substitute for supervised advanced performance training.

## Lecture 1: Lighting and visual composition

### Essential question
Which body segment initiates the movement, and what stabilizes it?

### 0–15 minutes — Conceptual lecture
Lighting directs attention through intensity, angle, color, timing, and contrast. A cue can reveal a gesture, flatten a spatial pattern, or obscure facial detail. A design proposal should distinguish artistic intent from technical feasibility and account for glare, heat, safe visibility, and performer orientation.

The central methodological challenge is to distinguish **observation**, **interpretation**, and **evaluation**. Observation describes events that can be checked against a movement score or performance. Interpretation explains possible significance in light of form, history, and context. Evaluation assesses a claim against criteria that must be stated. In the conservatory, the student should articulate each stage rather than treating a personal response as universal evidence. This discipline supports artistic freedom: the goal is not to prescribe one aesthetic, but to make artistic choices more precise and discussable.

### 15–30 minutes — Analytical demonstration
**Instructional focus:** Begin with the relevant foundational study note and a scholarly source listed in the bibliography; identify author, context, and limitations. Blocking diagram and cue sheet

Analyze the task along four dimensions: body action, spatial pathway, temporal organization, and expressive quality. Which dimension remains stable? Which changes? Consider at least two alternatives and predict their consequences. Make predictions before rehearsing, then distinguish outcomes from expectations. Where physical risk or consent is relevant, use a low-intensity adaptation or a written staging simulation rather than unsafe experimentation.

### 30–45 minutes — Studio laboratory
Annotate an eight-count phrase with three lighting cues and describe what each reveals or conceals.

**Movement material:** Counts 1–2 establish stance; 3–4 execute a comfortable version of the focus action; 5–6 repeat with a directional change; 7–8 recover and suspend.

**Creative transfer:** Apply lighting to Undercurrent's reach-and-gather motif. Document counts, direction, musical accent, and artistic intention.

Use the original *Undercurrent* motif—step and settle (1–2), gather (3–4), diagonal reach (5–6), suspend (7–8)—as a controlled reference phrase. Preserve its identity in the first version, alter one formal parameter in the second, and annotate what changed. At advanced levels, artistic complexity is not measured by how extreme a body position becomes.

### 45–55 minutes — Research seminar
Consult at least one source from the bibliography. Identify the author's question, method or critical framework, historical/cultural position, and limits of applicability. Ask whether a cited claim is empirical, interpretive, or professional guidance. Avoid treating a textbook's descriptive vocabulary as proof of a universal psychological response.

**Discussion:** Which body segment initiates the movement, and what stabilizes it? Explain two plausible positions and the evidence each would need. Describe one observation that would change your initial interpretation.

### 55–60 minutes — Retrieval and assessment
**Immediate recall:** Without consulting notes, define the central concept, apply it to the eight-count motif, and identify a limitation or alternative interpretation.

**Assignment:** Write 150–250 words answering: Which body segment initiates the movement, and what stabilizes it? Include an observation, a technical explanation, and one revision to the phrase.

**Evidence of learning:** Academic: correct terminology and source awareness. Technical: pain-free control, consistent rhythm and repeatable transitions. Creative: intentional and legible variation. No pass/fail on extreme range. Submit a short annotated movement score or staging plan, a written argument (300–500 words), and a revision memo. Academic rubric: precision 30%, reasoning and evidence 30%, contextual ethics 20%, clarity and revision 20%. Performance technique is evaluated separately by a qualified instructor where applicable. ## Lecture 2: Sightlines, blocking and audience geometry

### Essential question
How does the same action change with slower timing?

### 0–15 minutes — Conceptual lecture
Blocking establishes where performers travel and what audiences can see. Downstage, upstage, diagonals, and levels matter differently across thrust, arena, and proscenium arrangements. Set pieces and other dancers can occlude important moments. Sightline checks should be performed from multiple seats and accessibility locations.

## Lecture 3: Costume, props and movement safety

### Essential question
What would be an unsafe compensation or overgeneralization?

### 0–15 minutes — Conceptual lecture
Costume design changes silhouette, identity, sound, friction, and range of motion. Fabric may snag; footwear can change traction; loose accessories can obstruct vision or collide with others. Test costume and props under rehearsal conditions, not only in still photographs. Aesthetic cohesion does not justify preventable hazards.

## Lecture 4: Rehearsal leadership and cue communication

### Essential question
Which body segment initiates the movement, and what stabilizes it?

### 0–15 minutes — Conceptual lecture
Artistic direction requires clear roles, documented decisions, respectful feedback, and coordination across performers and technical teams. A cue sheet specifies the trigger, action, operator, and contingency. Feedback should be specific, consent-aware, and limited to observable work. Production meetings should record unresolved risks.

Production book. Compile the four unit submissions, an annotated 32-count movement score, a critical reflection on evidence and limits, and an artistic revision log. Demonstrate reliable retrieval and safe execution at your current level; never use maximal contortion or unsupervised inversions as an advancement requirement.
