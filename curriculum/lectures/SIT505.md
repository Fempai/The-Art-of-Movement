# SIT505 — Site-Specific & Cultural Performance

**Advanced conservatory seminar | Four 60-minute instructional lectures | Original course manuscript**

**Scope:** Space, audience, accessibility, context. **Expected learning:** Create a site-responsive concept ethically. These lectures contain timed instruction, interpretive frameworks, structured studio tasks, reading prompts, and assessed assignments. This is an independent nonaccredited curriculum. The lecture plans are not a substitute for supervised advanced performance training.

## Lecture 1: Space as choreographic material

### Essential question
How does cultural context affect the meaning of this technique?

### 0–15 minutes — Conceptual lecture
A site is not a neutral stage. Architecture, acoustic reflection, sightlines, textures, circulation routes, weather, and social use affect what can be performed and perceived. A site-responsive choreographer maps these constraints before making aesthetic claims. Consider the difference between staging a dance outdoors and designing a dance in response to a specific place.

The central methodological challenge is to distinguish **observation**, **interpretation**, and **evaluation**. Observation describes events that can be checked against a movement score or performance. Interpretation explains possible significance in light of form, history, and context. Evaluation assesses a claim against criteria that must be stated. In the conservatory, the student should articulate each stage rather than treating a personal response as universal evidence. This discipline supports artistic freedom: the goal is not to prescribe one aesthetic, but to make artistic choices more precise and discussable.

### 15–30 minutes — Analytical demonstration
**Instructional focus:** Begin with the relevant foundational study note and a scholarly source listed in the bibliography; identify author, context, and limitations. Map entrances, sightlines, surfaces, access needs

Analyze the task along four dimensions: body action, spatial pathway, temporal organization, and expressive quality. Which dimension remains stable? Which changes? Consider at least two alternatives and predict their consequences. Make predictions before rehearsing, then distinguish outcomes from expectations. Where physical risk or consent is relevant, use a low-intensity adaptation or a written staging simulation rather than unsafe experimentation.

### 30–45 minutes — Studio laboratory
Create a floor plan with entrances, exits, audience positions, obstacles, and a safe alternative route.

**Movement material:** Counts 1–2 establish stance; 3–4 execute a comfortable version of the focus action; 5–6 repeat with a directional change; 7–8 recover and suspend.

**Creative transfer:** Apply space to Undercurrent's reach-and-gather motif. Document counts, direction, musical accent, and artistic intention.

Use the original *Undercurrent* motif—step and settle (1–2), gather (3–4), diagonal reach (5–6), suspend (7–8)—as a controlled reference phrase. Preserve its identity in the first version, alter one formal parameter in the second, and annotate what changed. At advanced levels, artistic complexity is not measured by how extreme a body position becomes.

### 45–55 minutes — Research seminar
Consult at least one source from the bibliography. Identify the author's question, method or critical framework, historical/cultural position, and limits of applicability. Ask whether a cited claim is empirical, interpretive, or professional guidance. Avoid treating a textbook's descriptive vocabulary as proof of a universal psychological response.

**Discussion:** How does cultural context affect the meaning of this technique? Explain two plausible positions and the evidence each would need. Describe one observation that would change your initial interpretation.

### 55–60 minutes — Retrieval and assessment
**Immediate recall:** Without consulting notes, define the central concept, apply it to the eight-count motif, and identify a limitation or alternative interpretation.

**Assignment:** Write 150–250 words answering: How does cultural context affect the meaning of this technique? Include an observation, a technical explanation, and one revision to the phrase.

**Evidence of learning:** Academic: correct terminology and source awareness. Technical: pain-free control, consistent rhythm and repeatable transitions. Creative: intentional and legible variation. No pass/fail on extreme range. Submit a short annotated movement score or staging plan, a written argument (300–500 words), and a revision memo. Academic rubric: precision 30%, reasoning and evidence 30%, contextual ethics 20%, clarity and revision 20%. Performance technique is evaluated separately by a qualified instructor where applicable. This rubric has not been externally psychometrically validated.

## Lecture 2: Audience circulation and encounter

### Essential question
What do you notice when the musical accent moves?

### 0–15 minutes — Conceptual lecture
Proscenium spectators typically share a frontal viewing orientation; site-specific audiences may move, turn, or encounter action unexpectedly. Choreographic information can be obscured by crowding and sightline conflicts. Design multiple vantage points and ensure that important meaning does not depend solely on a privileged viewing position.

## Lecture 3: Accessibility as an artistic constraint

### Essential question
How can a novice reduce the task without losing its defining feature?

### 0–15 minutes — Conceptual lecture
Accessible performance requires more than a ramp: consider sensory load, seating, rest opportunities, captions or interpretive supports, navigation, surfaces, and the possibility of alternative ways to participate. Accessibility planning should involve the relevant community and avoid assuming a single accommodation works for everyone.

## Lecture 4: Context, permissions and ethics

### Essential question
How does cultural context affect the meaning of this technique?

### 0–15 minutes — Conceptual lecture
Public spaces can hold histories of exclusion, commemoration, labor, displacement, and community ownership. Permission to film or perform is distinct from ethical consultation. Consider whether the proposed choreography appropriates symbols, interrupts ordinary use, or exposes bystanders. Plan consent and privacy safeguards.

## Course-level capstone
Site-specific proposal. Compile the four unit submissions, an annotated 32-count movement score, a critical reflection on evidence and limits, and an artistic revision log. Demonstrate reliable retrieval and safe execution at your current level; never use maximal contortion or unsupervised inversions as an advancement requirement.
