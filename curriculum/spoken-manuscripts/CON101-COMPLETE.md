# CON101: Contemporary & Floorwork — Four spoken teaching manuscripts

**Original educational lecture scripts:** four lessons with full instructor narration, guided movement and seminar prompts. The scripts are not 60-minute verbatim transcripts; actual class length depends on practice and discussion. Sources are background references, not automatically evidence for every sentence.

## Spoken manuscript 1: Breath

**Instructional scope:** Contemporary & Floorwork. **Question:** Which quality distinguishes a deliberate artistic choice from an accident?

### Spoken introduction

"Welcome to today's conservatory seminar. Our focus is **breath**, a topic that becomes meaningful when we connect vocabulary to choices a dancer can actually make. I want you to think about a performance you remember. You probably recall the overall impression before the individual mechanics. Yet those mechanics—timing, placement, weight transfer, intention, and the way one action leads to another—are what let a performer build a repeatable experience. Our task is to make those decisions visible and discussable.

"Begin by considering this question: Which quality distinguishes a deliberate artistic choice from an accident? It is tempting to answer with a slogan. Instead, define the term, identify what would count as evidence, and distinguish the dancer's intention from an observer's interpretation. In an academic conservatory, a good answer is not simply confident. It is specific enough that another person could evaluate it, and honest enough to acknowledge uncertainty."

### Core teaching narrative

"Here is the movement problem I want us to investigate: Let breath inform the timing of a reach, but notice that breathing and spinal motion are not mechanically identical. Notice that this instruction has at least two layers. One is the observable event: where the body moves, when it moves, and what changes. The other is the reason for choosing that event. We can describe the first layer relatively directly; the second may require context, interviews, historical research, or an artist statement. The distinction matters because dancers often mistake a desired appearance for a technical explanation, or an emotional intention for a guaranteed audience response.

"In **Contemporary & Floorwork**, we will work toward this learning outcome: Control transitions between seated, kneeling and standing We should interpret that outcome as a capacity to explain and demonstrate a concept at an appropriate level, not as a demand for an extreme shape or a single preferred style. The useful question is what changes when one parameter changes. For example, a movement performed slowly may reveal transitions that fast execution conceals. A change in facing can alter the viewer's understanding without changing the dancer's underlying motif. And a movement performed in a new cultural context may require attribution and a different interpretive frame.

"To analyze today's material, use four questions. What moves? Where does it travel? When and with what rhythm does it unfold? How is its dynamic quality organized? These are descriptive lenses, not a claim that every dance form can be reduced to one vocabulary. Laban Movement Analysis provides one influential framework for Body, Effort, Space, and Shape; research on observational reliability reminds us that even trained observers can disagree. Use terminology to sharpen attention, not to manufacture certainty (How Shall I Count the Ways?, 2019; Assessing the Reliability of the Laban Movement Analysis System, 2019).

"Now consider a counterexample. Imagine that a dancer achieves the visible endpoint but arrives there with a different weight shift or timing. Is the task complete? The answer depends on the stated purpose. If we are measuring a final pose, perhaps the endpoint matters most. If we are teaching phrasing, musicality, or transition quality, the path is part of the result. If we are analyzing cultural history, the formal resemblance of two gestures does not establish a shared origin. Define the task before evaluating success."

### Guided demonstration and spoken coaching

"Stand or sit in a comfortable position and prepare to test the idea at a manageable level. Today's exercise is: Let breath inform the timing of a reach, but notice that breathing and spinal motion are not mechanically identical. I will demonstrate once while naming the movement and once while naming the musical counts. Your job is to identify what remains constant between the two versions. Do not increase range merely to make the difference more dramatic.

"Let's count the phrase. Counts 1–2 establish stance; 3–4 execute a comfortable version of the focus action; 5–6 repeat with a directional change; 7–8 recover and suspend. First, mark the pathway with a small gesture. Next, rehearse the weight transfer. Finally, combine it with the intended dynamic quality. Pause after each eight counts and tell me what you observed. Was the change in direction deliberate? Did the timing remain stable? Can you repeat the transition without copying a video? If not, reduce the task and rebuild it in smaller segments.

"Now we will connect the concept to our original repertory. Apply breath to Undercurrent's reach-and-gather motif. Document counts, direction, musical accent, and artistic intention. Imagine the reach-and-gather motif from *Undercurrent*: step and settle on counts one and two; gather on three and four; reach diagonally on five and six; recover and suspend on seven and eight. Keep this reference phrase stable, then alter just one parameter related to breath. We are not adding complexity for its own sake. We are making one artistic hypothesis and testing whether the audience could perceive the difference."

### Interpretation, evidence, and limitations

"Return to our essential question: Which quality distinguishes a deliberate artistic choice from an accident? What evidence do we actually have? Our studio exercise produces observations about this dancer, in this space, under these conditions. It does not prove a universal law. We must consider training history, fatigue, environment, musical genre, and the conventions of the dance tradition. A source from dance medicine may support a discussion of physical training but cannot automatically establish the history of a social dance. Likewise, an article about rhythm cannot settle a medical question. Select sources because they answer the claim you are making, not because their titles sound impressive.

"The accompanying reading is: Begin with the relevant foundational study note and a scholarly source listed in the bibliography; identify author, context, and limitations. The research links below provide background on movement observation, dance pedagogy, or rhythm. They are not interchangeable, and they should not be cited as direct evidence for a claim outside their scope. Before using a source in a paper, inspect the full text and note the author, method, population, and limits."

### Spoken conclusion and assignment

"Today we moved from a concept to an observable test, and from an observation to a defensible explanation. I want you to leave with a practical habit: name the variable you are changing and the evidence you would need to judge its effect. In your next practice session, reconstruct the phrase without prompts. If a transition disappears from memory, that is information about the learning process, not a verdict on your ability.

"Your assignment is: Write 150–250 words answering: Which quality distinguishes a deliberate artistic choice from an accident? Include an observation, a technical explanation, and one revision to the phrase. Your evaluation criteria are: Academic: correct terminology and source awareness. Technical: pain-free control, consistent rhythm and repeatable transitions. Creative: intentional and legible variation. No pass/fail on extreme range. In your written response, include a precise observation, one alternative explanation, a documented source relevant to your claim, and one concrete revision to the movement. Technical work should remain within a comfortable, controlled range and be assessed separately from scholarly reasoning."

**Discussion prompts:** (1) Which quality distinguishes a deliberate artistic choice from an accident? (2) What would count as a counterexample? (3) Which part of the conclusion depends on artistic interpretation rather than measurable movement?
