# LMA201 — Laban Movement Analysis: Four spoken instructional manuscripts

Original instructor-ready educational scripts with practical demonstration, seminar questions and documented reading leads. Each manuscript is designed for delivery with pauses and participation; it is not a 60-minute word-for-word transcript. Bibliography entries are background reading and must be checked for claim-specific relevance.

## Lecture 1 — Body

### Spoken opening

"Welcome back to Laban Movement Analysis. Today we will examine body through a question that connects theory with a decision you can make in the studio: How does cultural context affect the meaning of this technique? Think of an eight-count phrase you know. What would you have to change to make this concept visible, and what would you have to preserve to keep the phrase recognizable? We will investigate the idea as a dancer, a choreographer, and a critical observer.

"The important starting point is Body: name the segments and actions that actually move. That statement is a practical proposition, not a universal prescription. Our purpose is to explain the conditions in which it makes sense, identify what might challenge it, and translate it into movement. I want you to notice when you are describing an observable event and when you are explaining why you think it happened. The first can be checked against a video or score; the second requires evidence and careful interpretation."

### Spoken theory and examples

"Let's establish our terms. A movement phrase is not simply a series of poses. It unfolds through timing, changes in support, direction, and dynamic quality. The same endpoint can result from different paths. If you only remember the final picture, you may overlook what makes a phrase musical, safe, legible, or culturally specific. In our current topic, body becomes useful when we name what it changes in the dancer's task.

"Consider two versions of one movement. In the first, the performer makes a deliberate choice and can reproduce it. In the second, the same visible effect appears through an unplanned compensation or inconsistent transition. How would you tell them apart? You might compare timing, spatial pathway, support, a written score, or the dancer's account. No single observation is always decisive. In dance research and criticism, we should specify what we measured, what we inferred, and what we cannot know.

"The course's larger objective is this: Apply effort contrasts to the same motif Achieving that objective does not require a maximal range of motion or one preferred aesthetic. It requires an explanation that another person can understand and a demonstration appropriate to your training level. In technical work, a dancer's anatomy, conditioning, and fatigue matter. In historical and cultural work, context and community authority matter. In composition, formal coherence and deliberate variation matter. We must choose criteria that fit the question.

"One useful movement-analysis vocabulary asks four questions: what moves, where does it move, how does its quality change, and how does its form develop? Laban Movement Analysis offers Body, Space, Effort, and Shape as related lenses. This framework is valuable for describing movement, but research on its observational reliability cautions against pretending that every judgment is objective or that a particular dynamic proves a particular emotion. You may use the vocabulary as a tool while still questioning its assumptions and cultural reach."

### Spoken studio demonstration

"Now we will test the concept. Today's demonstration is: Body: name the segments and actions that actually move. First, mark it without striving for amplitude. Second, speak the counts while tracing the pathway. Third, execute the movement at a comfortable tempo. If you need a stable support for balance, use one. Avoid unstable equipment and stop when coordination or comfort deteriorates.

"Here is our teaching phrase: Counts 1–2 establish stance; 3–4 execute a comfortable version of the focus action; 5–6 repeat with a directional change; 7–8 recover and suspend. Repeat it twice. On the first repetition, prioritize accurate counts and weight shifts. On the second, vary one element relevant to body. Did the variation alter the phrase's identity, the audience's focus, or the effort required? Record an observation rather than immediately calling the change better or worse.

"Now transfer the idea to *Undercurrent*. Apply body to Undercurrent's reach-and-gather motif. Document counts, direction, musical accent, and artistic intention. Begin with the original reach-and-gather motif: step and settle on one and two, gather on three and four, diagonal reach on five and six, recover and suspend on seven and eight. Keep that version as a reference. Create a contrasting version, then explain precisely what changed. The aim is not to make a phrase more complicated. It is to make one choreographic decision visible and reproducible."

### Spoken research seminar

"Before we conclude, ask what the available evidence can support. Begin with the relevant foundational study note and a scholarly source listed in the bibliography; identify author, context, and limitations. The research and professional sources listed below offer frameworks for dance pedagogy, movement observation, rhythm, and scientific inquiry. None of them automatically proves a claim about every dance tradition or the meaning of our original choreography. Read the actual source and evaluate its question, method, context, and limitations. If you are discussing dance history, you will also need sources written with knowledge of the specific community; a generic movement-science article is insufficient.

"Consider a competing explanation. What if two dancers make the same movement look different because of anatomy, training, costume, camera angle, or musical context? What if two observers disagree about the expressive intention? A rigorous answer does not erase those differences. It makes the assumptions visible, proposes a way to check the observable parts, and respects the interpretive parts."

### Spoken closing and assessed work

"Return to the question: How does cultural context affect the meaning of this technique? You should now be able to define the idea, demonstrate or diagram it, and identify one limitation. I want you to practice explaining it aloud without your notes. Then reconstruct the eight-count phrase from memory, and make one revision based on what you noticed. The capacity to explain a movement is part of the capacity to teach, research, and create it.

"Your written task is: Write 150–250 words answering: How does cultural context affect the meaning of this technique? Include an observation, a technical explanation, and one revision to the phrase. The assessment criteria are: Academic: correct terminology and source awareness. Technical: pain-free control, consistent rhythm and repeatable transitions. Creative: intentional and legible variation. No pass/fail on extreme range. Include one clear observation, one interpretation with a reason, one relevant source, and one practical next step. Written reasoning and physical technique are separate dimensions of evaluation; no automatic score can certify the safety or artistry of your movement."

**Seminar questions:** How does cultural context affect the meaning of this technique? How would a competing theory explain the same movement? What evidence would change your conclusion?
