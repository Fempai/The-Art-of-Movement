# EST504 · Lesson 04 — Abstraction and the Evidence-Based Critical Essay

**Dance Criticism & Aesthetics | graduate-level independent study | 50–70 minutes.** Authored learner-facing revision. Scholarly citation audit, expert editorial review, and live-browser testing are outstanding.

## Critical framework

**Outcomes:** Distinguish description, interpretation, and evaluation; cite movement-specific evidence; recognize alternative readings; and critique the work without unsupported assumptions about a performer.

Abstract choreography need not present characters or a linear plot; it can organize movement through rhythm, contrast, repetition, spatial design, and changing dynamics. This does not mean abstract work has no meaning, nor that a narrative reading is forbidden. The critic's task is to distinguish formal organization from a possible interpretation. A narrative version of a phrase might foreground an imagined relationship through gaze and approach; an abstract version might foreground reach duration, pathway, and rhythmic displacement. Neither is inherently more advanced. A persuasive critical essay describes both versions closely, articulates a contestable thesis, considers an alternative interpretation, and avoids pretending that one response is universal.

## Guided performance-analysis studio

**Preparation:** Clear a safe area and mark a comfortable version of Undercurrent's step–gather–reach–recover motif. If performing is unsuitable, analyze an existing self-created score instead.

**Observe and compare (20–25 minutes):** Create two 32-count Undercurrent versions using the same safe movement vocabulary. Narrative version: direct gaze toward an imagined partner at the reach and withdraw on the recovery. Abstract version: preserve step–gather–reach–recover but vary the reach pathway in A′ and accent the return rather than implying a character. Keep a four-row score for A (1–8), B (9–16), A′ (17–24), C (25–32), noting exactly which counts changed. Film both from the same position or document each in an annotated score. Write a thesis comparing how focus and timing organize the two versions, then collect at least six count-specific observations.

**Revision and ethical check:** If you call the abstract version 'meaningless,' revise to describe what formal relationships are emphasized. If you claim the narrative version definitely communicates one story, qualify it as an intended or plausible reading. Avoid judging a recording feature as a choreographic flaw without checking framing.

**Evidence protocol:** Separate your notes into three headings: (1) what can be observed, with counts or timestamps; (2) what you infer and why; and (3) how you evaluate the choice under a stated criterion. A recording does not provide access to private emotions or universal audience reactions.

## Knowledge check

**1. What can abstract choreography emphasize?**

A. A sequence of movements that must always represent named fictional characters

B. Formal relationships such as timing, pathways, and repetition

C. A phrase whose meaning is established solely by a written plot synopsis

D. A movement structure that guarantees one emotional response across audiences

**2. Which is a defensible thesis?**

A. The gaze change proves that every viewer sees the same story

B. The phrase is abstract, so its timing and pathway cannot support interpretation

C. Changing gaze and accent shifts how I interpret the motif's organization

D. The accent change establishes that this genre is objectively superior

**3. What should a critical essay include?**

A. Count-specific evidence and a considered alternative reading

B. An overall reaction without citing any movement counts

C. A description of the story without observing the performed movement

D. Several sources listed without applying them to a specific observation

**4. What is the appropriate capstone claim?**

A. The analysis establishes how all viewers respond to the same phrase

B. The essay's detailed observations establish that the work is objectively superior

C. The interpretation reveals the performer's private thoughts from movement alone

D. The essay offers a situated, evidence-supported interpretation

### Answer key and rationale

**1. B.** Abstract choreography can emphasize relationships among timing, pathways, repetition, and spatial organization. It need not depict named characters (A), depend entirely on a plot synopsis (C), or guarantee one emotional response from every audience member (D).

**2. C.** Connecting changed gaze and accent to an interpretation of motif organization offers a testable, situated argument. Universal reception (A), the claim that abstraction prevents interpretation (B), and objective genre superiority (D) are unsupported overstatements.

**3. A.** Count-specific observations plus a considered alternative interpretation make an essay's argument traceable and contestable. Reaction without movement evidence (B), story-only summary (C), and unintegrated source lists (D) do not demonstrate analysis of the performed work.

**4. D.** A critical essay can offer a well-supported interpretation while stating its evidentiary and contextual limits. It cannot establish all viewers' reactions (A), objective artistic superiority (B), or a performer's unspoken private thoughts (C) from movement alone.

**Self-correction:** For missed items, rewrite one unsupported assertion as a situated, evidence-supported claim. These questions are instructional, not a validated critical-writing examination.

## Independent critical assignment

Produce a 300–450-word analysis containing a thesis, at least four movement-specific observations, an alternative reading, and a source-limit statement. For Lesson 04, submit a **1,000–1,300-word critical essay** comparing narrative and abstract versions of the 32-count Undercurrent phrase, with six count-specific observations, a clear thesis, relevant verified scholarship, ethical attribution, and a conclusion identifying the limits of your interpretation.

**Worked example:** “The diagonal reach lasts two counts in A but three in A′. I read the added duration as a moment of uncertainty, although another viewer could interpret it as resolve. The timing contrast is observable; the emotion is my interpretation.”

**Rubric:** Accuracy and specificity of movement description 30%; reasoning and evidence 30%; engagement with alternatives and context 20%; ethical attribution, structure, and source transparency 20%. No credit for fabricated citations or presumed performer psychology.
