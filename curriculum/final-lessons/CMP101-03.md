# CMP101 · Lesson 03 — Transitions: Connect Phrases Without Losing the Idea

**Independent composition study | 40–55 minutes.** Learner-facing revision; source metadata, live links, and browser acceptance remain pending. The site serves as your teacher for these foundational tasks, but no self-score guarantees how an outside viewer will interpret a work.

## Concept and learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to define the key compositional idea, apply it to an original phrase, record it precisely enough to repeat, and explain what the notation does and does not show.

A transition is the movement or compositional event that connects one phrase, level, facing, or idea to another. It may be a step, a weight transfer, a pause, or a gradual redirection. A transition is not necessarily filler: it can establish causality, contrast, anticipation, or recovery. When a transition feels awkward, check whether the ending weight and facing of phrase A match the starting requirements of phrase B. For independent practice, map *exit state* and *entry state* explicitly before adding expressive detail.

**Observation versus intention:** You can verify that an arm reaches on count five. You cannot prove that every viewer reads that reach as longing. Write the intended effect separately from the count and pathway.

## Guided studio and writing lab

**Preparation (3–5 minutes):** Clear a nonslip space, warm up with gentle steps or seated torso shifts, and decide which direction is the front of the room. Use a small comfortable range; avoid painful or unstable movement.

**Follow this score (10–15 minutes):** Perform Phrase A: 1 step right; 2 settle weight right; 3–4 gather; 5–6 reach right; 7 lower; 8 remain weighted right. Phrase B begins with a leftward traveling step on count 1. Transition version 1: on count 8, keep weight right so the left foot is free for B count 1. Version 2: shift weight left on count 8, then notice that B count 1 requires a preparatory change. **Concrete no-extra-count repair for version 2:** keep the left-weighted exit on A count 8, but change B count 1 to a **right-foot** step and record that B's entry pathway is now different. **Alternative with the original left-foot entry:** rescore A count 8 as a supported shift back to the right, freeing the left foot for B count 1. If you instead insert a preparatory count, explicitly rescore the boundary and total duration. Do not add an unmarked beat. Do not force an off-balance step to preserve a written plan. **Seated adaptation:** On a stable nonrolling chair with both feet grounded, mark A counts 1–7 with a small rightward pressure shift, gather, rightward reach, and lower; on A count 8 keep seated pressure right for version 1, then mark B count 1 with a small leftward hand reach. For version 2, shift seated pressure left on A count 8, then either mark B count 1 with a rightward hand reach or revise A count 8 to shift pressure right before a leftward hand reach. This demonstrates planning of support and direction, not a literal standing free-foot test.

**Compare and revise:** If the free foot needed for the next step is already bearing weight, the choreography may contain an unscored transfer. Correct the score rather than assuming you have a coordination defect.

**Undercurrent connection:** Use the existing step–gather–reach–recover motif as material. Identify what you kept, changed, and why. Record two takes or write two complete count sheets. A single photograph of the endpoint is insufficient to document timing or support.

**Self-check:** Can you perform the phrase twice with the same counts and orientation? Does your score identify the support foot and the pathway? If you cannot reconstruct it, revise the notation before increasing complexity.

## Knowledge check

**1. What is an exit state?**

A. The movement pathway planned for the next phrase rather than the state reached

B. The support and orientation at a phrase's end

C. The emotion the choreographer wants the audience to feel at the ending

D. The first movement of the next phrase without recording the current support

**2. Why can a transition feel awkward?**

A. The exit and entry support requirements may conflict

B. The dancer repeats the same support and facing through the boundary without changing counts

C. Both phrases share the same support and facing, so the transition requires no adjustment

D. The dancer uses a comfortable pause to prepare the next phrase's required support

**3. What is a useful repair if the next step needs a free left foot?**

A. Keep the left foot weighted while attempting to move it immediately

B. Preserve the left-foot support and change only the arm pathway

C. Place weight on the right before that step

D. Rotate the torso while leaving the required left-foot support unchanged

**4. Which statement about transitions is accurate?**

A. They matter only when a phrase contains a jump, not during weight transfers

B. They must always occupy a separate count between two phrases

C. They preserve the exact same support and facing in every composition

D. They can contribute meaning and structure

### Answer key and rationale

**1. B.** The exit state records the support and orientation reached at a phrase's end. A describes a future pathway, C describes intended audience response, and D skips the current support information required to plan a safe entry.

**2. A.** An awkward transition may arise when the first phrase ends with support needed by the next step, or when orientations conflict. B and C describe compatible states rather than a conflict, while D describes a deliberate preparation that may help resolve one.

**3. C.** Shifting weight onto the right foot before the entry frees the left foot for its planned step. A leaves the moving foot loaded, B changes the arms without addressing support, and D changes torso orientation without freeing the left foot. Use a comfortable supported transfer if needed.

**4. D.** Transitions connect phrase states and can contribute to rhythm, structure, and expressive intention. A ignores ordinary weight transfers, B invents a mandatory extra count, and C incorrectly requires unchanged support and facing.

**Retry:** Revisit any missed concept, annotate the movement example, and answer again without looking. These are instructor-authored conceptual checks, not externally validated tests of choreographic talent.

## Independent assignment and rubric

Create an annotated composition score. Include count numbers, action verbs, support foot or seated support, facing, pathway, at least one intentional change, and a 250–350-word critical reflection. For Lesson 03, submit two versions of the A-to-B boundary, identifying the support and facing at A's exit and B's entry; explain how you resolved any conflicting weight-transfer requirement.

**Worked example:** “**Version 1:** At A count 8, my weight stays on the right, leaving the left foot free to step left on B count 1; I record the facing as front at both sides of the boundary. **Version 2:** At A count 8, I transfer weight left, so I change B count 1 to a **right-foot** step toward the right while keeping the same front facing. This changes B's entry pathway but needs no extra beat. If I want B to retain its original left-foot step instead, I must rescore A count 8 as a rightward support transfer. I document which repair I actually performed rather than writing an impossible left-foot entry from a fully left-weighted exit.”

**Rubric:** Structural clarity 25%; precise, reproducible notation 25%; intentional variation or contrast 25%; critical reflection and source awareness 25%. A self-assessment should identify a concrete revision, not merely give a high score.
