# CMP101 · Lesson 03 — Transitions: Connect Phrases Without Losing the Idea

**Independent composition study | 40–55 minutes.** Learner-facing revision; source metadata, live links, and browser acceptance remain pending. The site serves as your teacher for these foundational tasks, but no self-score guarantees how an outside viewer will interpret a work.

## Concept and learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to define the key compositional idea, apply it to an original phrase, record it precisely enough to repeat, and explain what the notation does and does not show.

A transition is the movement or compositional event that connects one phrase, level, facing, or idea to another. It may be a step, a weight transfer, a pause, or a gradual redirection. A transition is not necessarily filler: it can establish causality, contrast, anticipation, or recovery. When a transition feels awkward, check whether the ending weight and facing of phrase A match the starting requirements of phrase B. For independent practice, map *exit state* and *entry state* explicitly before adding expressive detail.

**Observation versus intention:** You can verify that an arm reaches on count five. You cannot prove that every viewer reads that reach as longing. Write the intended effect separately from the count and pathway.

## Guided studio and writing lab

**Preparation (3–5 minutes):** Clear a nonslip space, warm up with gentle steps or seated torso shifts, and decide which direction is the front of the room. Use a small comfortable range; avoid painful or unstable movement.

**Follow this score (10–15 minutes):** Perform Phrase A: 1 step right; 2 settle weight right; 3–4 gather; 5–6 reach right; 7 lower; 8 remain weighted right. Phrase B begins with a leftward traveling step on count 1. Transition version 1: on count 8, keep weight right so the left foot is free for B count 1. Version 2: shift weight left on count 8, then notice that B count 1 requires a preparatory change. Decide whether to add a count, alter B, or redesign the transition. Do not force an off-balance step to preserve a written plan.

**Compare and revise:** If the free foot needed for the next step is already bearing weight, the choreography may contain an unscored transfer. Correct the score rather than assuming you have a coordination defect.

**Undercurrent connection:** Use the existing step–gather–reach–recover motif as material. Identify what you kept, changed, and why. Record two takes or write two complete count sheets. A single photograph of the endpoint is insufficient to document timing or support.

**Self-check:** Can you perform the phrase twice with the same counts and orientation? Does your score identify the support foot and the pathway? If you cannot reconstruct it, revise the notation before increasing complexity.

## Knowledge check

**1. What is an exit state?**

A. The support and orientation at a phrase's end

B. The movement pathway planned for the next phrase rather than the state reached

C. The emotion the choreographer wants the audience to feel at the ending

D. The first movement of the next phrase without recording the current support

**2. Why can a transition feel awkward?**

A. The exit and entry support requirements may conflict

B. The phrases may demand different support feet at their shared boundary

C. The ending facing may differ from the next phrase's required entry facing

D. The outgoing count and incoming count may require incompatible weight shifts

**3. What is a useful repair if the next step needs a free left foot?**

A. Place weight on the right before that step

B. Keep the left foot weighted while attempting to move it immediately

C. Preserve the left-foot support and change only the arm pathway

D. Rotate the torso while leaving the required left-foot support unchanged

**4. Which statement about transitions is accurate?**

A. They can contribute meaning and structure

B. They matter only when a phrase contains a jump, not during weight transfers

C. They must always occupy a separate count between two phrases

D. They preserve the exact same support and facing in every composition

### Answer key and rationale

**1. A.** The exit state describes how a phrase leaves the dancer positioned.

**2. A.** Support and facing mismatches are common compositional problems.

**3. A.** Keeping the left foot available makes the intended step more reproducible.

**4. A.** Transitions can be expressive and structurally important.

**Retry:** Revisit any missed concept, annotate the movement example, and answer again without looking. These are instructor-authored conceptual checks, not externally validated tests of choreographic talent.

## Independent assignment and rubric

Create an annotated composition score. Include count numbers, action verbs, support foot or seated support, facing, pathway, at least one intentional change, and a 250–350-word critical reflection. For Lesson 04, submit a complete **32-count A–B–A′–C score**.

**Worked example:** “Counts 3–4: both hands gather toward the sternum; weight remains on the right foot. Counts 5–6: right arm reaches northeast. In A′, the reach lasts through count 7. The duration changed; my intended meaning is an interpretation, not an objective property of the score.”

**Rubric:** Structural clarity 25%; precise, reproducible notation 25%; intentional variation or contrast 25%; critical reflection and source awareness 25%. A self-assessment should identify a concrete revision, not merely give a high score.
