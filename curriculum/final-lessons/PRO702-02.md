# PRO702 · Lesson 02 — Sightlines and Blocking: Compose for the Viewer

**Stagecraft & Artistic Direction | independent production study | 50–70 minutes.** Authored learner-facing revision. Scholarly passage verification, expert production review, and live browser testing remain pending.

## Production principle

**Outcomes:** Explain a stagecraft variable, plan it with a diagram or cue sheet, test an observable effect safely, and document a revision.

Blocking specifies where a performer stands, travels, faces, and exits at each point in a work. Sightlines describe what a viewer can see from a particular position. A blocking plan needs a coordinate system and a clear distinction between dancer right/left and audience right/left. In a small room, blocking can be performed without travel by changing facing and reach direction. Do not claim a sightline is unobstructed merely because the performer sees the camera; the viewer's height, distance, and obstructions matter. Stage diagrams are planning tools, not proof of audience reception.

## Guided production laboratory

**Prepare:** Use a level, clear, permitted rehearsal area. Keep exits unobstructed. Do not install lighting equipment, alter wiring, climb rigging, or use hazardous props.

**Plan and test (20–30 minutes):** Draw a rectangular stage map with upstage at the top, downstage at the bottom, and audience front at the bottom edge. Mark three positions: A = downstage center, B = center, C = upstage center, but choose safe locations that are genuinely clear. Score Undercurrent A (1–8) facing front at A, B (9–16) facing a comfortable diagonal at B, A′ (17–24) returning front, and C (25–32) finishing at C. **The map describes desired section locations, not extra travel counts: either assign any safe travel to the existing scored transitions and mark exact counts, or keep the entire phrase at A and change only gaze/arm facing. Never silently add steps between sections.** If travel is unsafe, keep one position and annotate facing changes instead. Draw sightlines from front-center and front-side hypothetical viewers; note where an arm could overlap the torso. Rehearse the 32 counts once and correct the map to reflect actual movement.

**Accessible modification:** If travel is unsafe or inaccessible, keep a stable seated or supported position and mark A, B, and C as hypothetical positions on the stage map. Test facing and arm visibility from that fixed position, and clearly distinguish observed sightlines from the untested travel design.

**Troubleshooting and safety:** If a travel route intersects furniture, eliminate the travel. If an arm is hidden from the side viewpoint, test a small change in facing rather than increasing range. Always label whether a sightline is observed or predicted.

**Undercurrent application:** Link each staging choice to the original step–gather–reach–recover score. Record what the audience might be able to see, but distinguish a visibility prediction from tested reception.

## Knowledge check

**1. What does blocking document?**

A. The performer's intended emotional state without describing their stage position

B. Positions, pathways, facing, and entrances/exits

C. The sequence of musical cues without performer pathways

D. The costume silhouette without marking entrances or facing

**2. What belongs on a usable stage diagram?**

A. A drawing of the stage shape without a front-of-stage reference

B. A list of performer names without any placement coordinates

C. Orientation labels and performer positions

D. A scale-free sketch with no direction labels or entry points

**3. What should happen when planned travel crosses an obstacle?**

A. Remove or redesign the travel

B. Keep the pathway and ask the performer to step over the obstacle faster

C. Rehearse the original route without recording the obstruction

D. Keep the obstacle and rely on the performer remembering its location

**4. What can a floor plan alone not establish?**

A. The stage-front direction shown on the diagram

B. The planned entrance marked on the diagram

C. The position specified for the performer at count one

D. What every actual spectator will see or feel

### Correct answers and explanations

**1. B.** Blocking specifies performer locations, pathways, facing, and entrances or exits so movement can be reproduced in the space. Emotional intention alone (A), music cues without travel (C), and costume appearance without placement (D) are not substitutes for spatial instructions.

**2. C.** A usable stage diagram identifies orientation and performer positions, making its directions interpretable. A shape without stage-front reference (A), names without positions (B), and an unlabeled sketch (D) do not supply enough spatial information.

**3. A.** Remove the obstruction or redesign the pathway before rehearsing travel so the performer can move without crossing a hazard. Stepping over it faster (B), ignoring it in the record (C), or relying only on memory (D) leaves the obstacle risk unresolved.

**4. D.** A floor plan can show intended stage front (A), planned entrances (B), and a count-one position (C). It cannot demonstrate what every real spectator will see or feel; sightlines and audience interpretation require separate evidence.

**Self-review:** For any missed item, revise the relevant blocking, lighting, or risk-control entry. These original questions check planning concepts, not technical certification.

## Independent assignment

Submit a labeled stage map (upstage/downstage, audience orientation and dancer-right/left legend) with safe positions A = downstage center, B = center, C = upstage center. Score the taught Undercurrent blocking and reach directions by count, identify at least two hypothetical viewing positions, and record a sightline/obstruction check from each without claiming audience approval. Write a 250–350-word rationale for one count-specific facing or pathway revision while keeping exits clear.

**Worked example:** “From the front-left viewing position, the torso hid the reach on count 5. I adjusted the facing for counts 5–6 and revised the map, distinguishing the tested camera viewpoint from hypothetical audience visibility.”

**Rubric:** Correctly oriented blocking map 30%; count-specific, executable score 30%; sightline evidence and limits 25%; safe revision and rationale 15%.
