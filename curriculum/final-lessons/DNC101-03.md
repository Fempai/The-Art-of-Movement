## What you will learn

## Lesson: read before practicing

Relevé means 'raised' and commonly refers to rising onto the balls of the feet in ballet. In a beginner two-foot relevé, the ankle plantarflexors contribute to elevation while the feet and lower limbs manage balance. It is different from simply curling the toes or jumping. A small, supported rise retains the defining action without requiring high elevation or single-leg balance. Jazz technique also uses elevated positions, but their phrasing and aesthetics can differ. The important beginner skill is to rise and lower with deliberate timing and stable foot contact.

**Why this matters:** In a self-taught setting, a name alone is not instruction. You need to know which foot supports your weight, what pathway the working limb takes, and what would count as a repeatable action. A shape that resembles a photograph is not proof that you used the intended movement. A slower tempo often helps you observe transitions, but it is not automatically the correct performance tempo for every style.

**Preparation:** Stand near a wall or securely fixed surface, feet parallel and comfortably apart. Keep fingertips on the support as needed. Avoid standing on a slippery surface or using a rolling object for balance. Begin with 3–5 minutes of comfortable marching, ankle circles, and small knee bends. Do not force range, and stop if you experience pain, dizziness, numbness, or repeated loss of control.

## Follow-along movement instruction

- **Count 1–2: Press through the balls of both feet and raise the heels a small distance.**
- **Count 3–4: Hold briefly while breathing normally and keeping weight comfortably distributed.**
- **Count 5–6: Lower the heels smoothly, without dropping.**
- **Count 7: Return to a comfortable neutral stance.**
- **Count 8: Pause and check whether the foot pressure felt even and controllable.**

**Repeat the phrase:** Perform it three times at a comfortable pace. On the first repetition, say the counts. On the second, attend to foot contact and support. On the third, make the phrasing intentional without increasing the range.

**Correct-versus-misleading comparison:** Rising higher is not always a better relevé. If the ankles wobble or the toes grip excessively, the task is too demanding at that range. A low supported rise is a valid progression.

**Transfer to Undercurrent:** Use the original eight-count step–gather–reach–recover motif. Replace the gather or transition with a small version of today's movement, then explain whether the change affects timing, support, or the audience's visual focus. You are composing an original study, not copying choreography from a source.

## Troubleshooting when practicing alone

| What you notice | What might be happening | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| Your ankles roll outward or inward | Balance or load may exceed your current control | Lower the height and use a fixed support |
| Your heels drop abruptly | You may be rushing the lowering phase | Use two full counts to descend |
| You grip your toes and hold your breath | You may be over-efforting | Reduce height and maintain comfortable breathing |

**Success check:** You can identify your support foot, demonstrate the movement twice at the same counts in a comfortable range, and explain one difference between the intended pathway and a common error. This does not certify injury safety or advanced technique.

## Knowledge check — try before reading the answers

**1. A dancer begins with both feet grounded, then lifts the heels while maintaining controlled support through the forefeet. Which action are they practicing?**

A. Rising onto the balls of the feet

B. Transferring weight to the side while both heels remain down

C. Flexing the knees while keeping both heels grounded

D. Extending one foot along the floor while the standing heel stays down

**2. A learner cannot repeat a high unsupported relevé without wobbling. Which alternative keeps the central rising action?**

A. A small two-foot rise with stable support

B. A two-foot high rise without support, keeping the original difficult height

C. A small supported knee bend with both heels remaining grounded

D. A supported foot slide with no heel rise

**3. A learner reaches the top of the rise but drops their heels abruptly. What does a slower descent help them study?**

A. To rehearse control through the descent

B. Whether the peak height increases without changing the descent

C. Whether the dancer can pause at the top without practicing the lowering

D. Whether the foot can slide farther along the floor

**4. The ankles wobble on the third repetition despite an accurate first rise. What adjustment best preserves the beginner objective?**

A. Repeat at the same height while speeding up the lowering

B. Reduce the range and use support

C. Keep the same height and move weight onto one foot

D. Omit the lowering phase while keeping the unstable rise

### Answers and explanations

**1. A.** Relevé involves controlled elevation through the foot and ankle.

**2. A.** A supported low rise preserves the defining action.

**3. A.** Controlled lowering is a meaningful part of the skill.

**4. B.** Scaling the range and adding support can make practice more manageable.

## Assignment and worked example

**Task:** Perform three low supported relevés and three controlled lowerings. Record whether the movement is smoother when you use a two-count descent versus a four-count descent. Write 250–350 words explaining your movement choices, what you observed, what you could not verify, and one source-supported principle relevant to the exercise.

**Worked example:** “With one hand on a fixed support, I raised both heels slightly over two counts and lowered them over two counts. When I used four counts to descend, I could pay more attention to even foot pressure. This observation does not prove my ankles are stronger or that the movement is safe for everyone.”

**Rubric:** Terminology and accuracy 25%; counts and movement description 25%; analysis and revision 25%; appropriate source use and limitations 25%. The rubric evaluates academic reasoning separately from technical skill.
