# CON101 · Lesson 03 — Suspension, Delay, and Release

**Self-directed contemporary dance | 35–50 minutes including movement, retrieval, and reflection.** No instructor or barre required for the foundational version. Advanced floorwork, inversions, falls, and painful movements require qualified instruction. Live browser acceptance and claim-level source auditing are still pending.

## Learning goal and core explanation

After this lesson, explain the concept, demonstrate a controlled low-risk version, compare two intentional choices, and document a movement phrase.

Suspension in choreography can describe a perceptible delay before a movement resolves, such as holding a reach before transferring weight or allowing an arm to linger before lowering. It does not require jumping or literally floating. In dance analysis, a pause can be active: the dancer still manages balance, muscular tension, and anticipation. The artistic effect depends on timing, musical context, and the viewer; it is not a guaranteed emotional response.

**Think like a choreographer:** What do you actually observe—timing, direction, weight, contact points—and what are you inferring, such as the dancer's emotion or intention? A strong interpretation states its evidence and acknowledges alternatives.

## Follow-along studio instruction

**Warm up for 3–5 minutes:** Walk or march gently, move shoulders and ankles through comfortable ranges, and practice an easy weight shift. Clear the floor, avoid slippery socks or moving furniture, and keep a fixed support available if needed. Stop for pain, dizziness, numbness, or repeated loss of control.

**Guided eight-count study:** Begin with both feet grounded. On 1–2 shift weight slightly right; on 3–4 extend the left arm diagonally; on 5 hold the arm's endpoint while breathing normally; on 6 begin returning; on 7–8 settle to neutral. Repeat without the count-5 pause. Compare the two versions. For a more advanced but still low-risk variation, let the heels remain grounded while you shift the torso slightly forward, then recover. Do not jump, fall, or hold a strained balance.

**Independent practice:** Perform the sequence once while speaking counts, once while observing the initiation or support pathway, and once with a deliberate change of timing. If a version becomes unstable, reduce the range or use the seated alternative.

**Undercurrent application:** A sustained reach on count 5 may make the next return more noticeable. Whether a viewer reads the pause as uncertainty, hope, or something else remains interpretive. Use the original step–gather–reach–recover motif, then change only one variable associated with this lesson. Write down the eight counts and describe the difference without claiming a universal audience reaction.

## Troubleshooting without a teacher

| What happens | Possible explanation, not diagnosis | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| You lock joints during the pause | You may be equating stillness with rigidity | Reduce reach and keep the posture comfortably active |
| You fall out of the pause | The balance demand may be too high | Keep both feet grounded and shorten the hold |
| The pause disappears at full tempo | You may not have allocated a distinct count to it | Speak counts 4–5–6 and mark the hold before adding arms |

**Ready to move on when:** You can repeat the comfortable version twice at a steady count, name the supporting surface or foot, and explain one deliberate choreographic change. This is a learning checkpoint, not medical clearance.

## Knowledge check

**1. What can suspension mean in this lesson?**

A. A continuous reach that resolves without any timing delay

B. A deliberate delay before resolving movement

C. A change in facing without a change in timing or resolution

D. A rapid release into the next action without a perceptible hold

**2. What is the clearest way to test the effect of suspension?**

A. Compare two phrases that also change the tempo and pathway

B. Compare two different arm shapes without changing their timing

C. Compare a phrase with and without a deliberate pause

D. Repeat the same hold twice without a contrasting version

**3. What should a beginner do if a held reach causes wobbling?**

A. Keep both feet grounded and reduce the hold

B. Keep the same unsupported reach but shorten only the music

C. Extend the hold while shifting weight farther from the base

D. Repeat the same unstable hold faster to reach the next count

**4. Which claim is an interpretation rather than direct observation?**

A. The dancer paused on count five

B. The right arm remained extended

C. The feet stayed on the floor

D. The pause was intended to communicate grief and must be perceived that way by every viewer

### Answer key and explanations

**1. B.** A timed hold or delay can create a suspended quality.

**2. C.** A controlled comparison isolates the timing change.

**3. A.** A smaller supported version preserves the timing idea.

**4. D.** The intended or perceived emotion cannot be established from the timing alone.

**Self-grading:** Four correct indicates understanding of the introductory distinctions tested here. For a missed answer, revisit its explanation and demonstrate the related idea at a smaller range before retrying. These original questions are not externally validated examinations.

## Independent assignment

Create two eight-count versions of the same phrase. Record the count, initiating segment, direction, support, and intended effect. Write 250–350 words comparing the versions, identifying one observable difference, one interpretation, and one limitation. Include at least one relevant reference and explain what that source can and cannot support.

**Worked example:** “In version A, I reached for two counts and returned immediately. In version B, I held the reach for one additional count. The duration changed, which I can verify by counting; whether that pause suggests hesitation is an interpretation.”

**Rubric:** Concept and terminology 25%; accurate movement score 25%; comparison and critical reasoning 25%; source use and revision 25%. Written grades do not establish the safety or quality of unsupervised advanced technique.
