# CMP502 · Lesson 04 — Fragmentation, Chance, and a 2–4 Minute Original Work

**Advanced Composition | graduate-level creative inquiry | 50–70 minutes.** This is an authored learner-facing revision; bibliographic audit, expert review, and browser acceptance testing remain pending.

## Lecture: define the compositional device

**Learning goals:** Explain the featured device, construct a reproducible movement score, identify its difference from simple repetition, and justify a revision without claiming the resulting work has a universal emotional meaning.

Fragmentation extracts and rearranges selected parts of a movement motif; chance procedures assign some compositional decisions using a defined random mechanism. These are distinct: a fragment can be chosen intentionally, and chance can select from complete phrases. A transparent chance score names the eligible options, how they are selected, and which constraints remain fixed for safety and coherence. Chance does not remove the choreographer's responsibility for editing, attribution, or safe execution. A 2–4 minute composition needs a timing plan; four eight-count phrases alone will not fill that duration at ordinary tempos.

## Guided choreography laboratory

**Prepare (5 minutes):** Warm up with comfortable walking or seated shifts and gentle arm movements. Use an open nonslip area. No falls, forced ranges, or unsupported inversions are required.

**Build and rehearse (20 minutes):** Take Undercurrent's four events: step, gather, reach, return. Create fragments F1 = gather+reach (4 counts), F2 = step+return (4 counts), F3 = reach alone with sustain (4 counts), F4 = small weight shift+gather (4 counts). Write each on a card, shuffle, and draw four cards with replacement to make a 16-count module; record the draw order before rehearsing. Review each boundary for support and add a safe transition if necessary, keeping the count sheet accurate. To build a 2-minute work at 80 beats/minute, plan approximately 160 counts (five 32-count sections); at 80 beats/minute 160 beats is 120 seconds. Use sections: introduction (32), motif (32), retrograde (32), accumulation or canon-inspired layering (32), and chance-fragment resolution (32). If tempo differs, recalculate total counts and document actual duration. Rehearse sections separately before full run.

**Troubleshooting and critical editing:** If a random order creates a hazardous movement, redraw or modify it and record the intervention. If the work is only 32 counts, do not label it two minutes without a tempo and timing calculation. If using a layered canon, state clearly whether the second part is a recording rather than a live second dancer.

**Analytical comparison:** Record the original motif and its transformation in separate columns: event, count, pathway, support, and changed compositional rule. Explain which elements remain recognizable and why. If a video is unavailable, a detailed count score is sufficient.

## Knowledge check

**1. What is fragmentation?**

A. Performing the complete motif faster without selecting or rearranging parts

B. Extracting and reorganizing parts of a motif

C. Reversing every event in the full motif without extracting a fragment

D. Repeating the original motif at the same tempo and event order

**2. What must a chance procedure specify?**

A. A list of possible movements without stating how one will be selected

B. Options, selection rule, and constraints

C. A random number result without documenting the choices it represented

D. A selection rule without stating any limits on hazardous transitions

**3. At 80 beats/minute, how long are 160 beats?**

A. 80 seconds, assuming one beat lasts half a second

B. 100 seconds, treating 80 BPM as 96 BPM

C. 120 seconds

D. 160 seconds, assuming one second per beat

**4. What is an ethical and safe response to a hazardous random transition?**

A. Follow the generated sequence even when the transition lacks stable support

B. Replace the hazardous transition without recording the intervention

C. Keep the original draw because randomness removes the choreographer's responsibility

D. Modify or redraw and document the intervention

### Answer key with reasoning

**1. B.** Fragmentation extracts identifiable parts of a source motif and reorganizes them into a new arrangement. Merely speeding up the whole motif (A), reversing the entire motif without selecting fragments (C), or repeating it unchanged (D) does not perform the taught extraction.

**2. B.** Documenting the available options, the selection rule, and safety constraints makes a chance-based composition repeatable and inspectable. Options without a draw rule (A), a result without its mapping (C), or a rule that ignores hazardous transitions (D) is incomplete.

**3. C.** At 80 beats per minute, 160 beats take 160/80 = 2 minutes, or 120 seconds. Eighty seconds (A), 100 seconds (B), and 160 seconds (D) all use a beat duration inconsistent with the stated tempo.

**4. D.** Modify or redraw a hazardous random transition and document the intervention so both the safety decision and chance procedure remain transparent. Performing an unstable transition (A), concealing the change (B), or treating randomness as permission to ignore risk (C) is not acceptable.

**Self-review:** For any missed item, correct the compositional rule and demonstrate the difference in a smaller four-count version. These questions are original instructional checks, not a validated professional assessment.

## Independent assignment

Create a **2–4 minute original composition** integrating at least three explicitly named devices studied in this course (retrograde, canon-inspired layering, accumulation, fragmentation, or chance). Submit a section-by-section count score, device rules, source motif, safe transition edits, rehearsal log, and artist statement with a 250–350-word critical reflection. For a 2-minute version at 80 BPM, score five 32-count sections (160 beats = 120 seconds); if your chosen tempo or duration differs, recalculate. For the chance section, document the eligible fragment cards, draw-with-replacement rule, actual draw order, and any safety redraw. Distinguish solo simulation or recorded layers from live group canon. A private video is optional; the score must stand alone.

**Worked example:** “At 80 BPM I planned 160 beats in five 32-count sections: introduction, motif, retrograde, accumulation, and chance-fragment resolution. My chance module uses four four-count draws, with replacement, from F1–F4; I wrote down the resulting order before rehearsal and repeated or developed the 16-count module within the final section. I documented a boundary adjustment rather than concealing an unsafe draw. The complete count sheet totals 160 beats, so the planned duration is 120 seconds, excluding any unscored pause.”

**Rubric:** Transparent use of at least three devices 30%; complete count-level duration and section structure 25%; safe, executable transitions and honest chance documentation 25%; rehearsal critique, reflection, and artist statement 20%. The self-score is not a professional adjudication.
