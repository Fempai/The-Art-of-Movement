# MOV101 · Lesson 02 — Muscular Actions, Gravity, and Tempo

**Learner-facing self-study lesson.** Approximate session: 35–50 minutes including practice, retrieval, and writing. Requires no ballet barre. Source links and metadata are included, but live browser and authenticated learner testing remain outstanding.

## Learning outcomes

After completing this lesson, you should be able to explain concentric, eccentric, and isometric muscle actions; identify why slow lowering can demand control; distinguish muscle function from visual appearance.

## Read and understand

A muscle creates tension, but the visible motion depends on joint position, gravity, external forces, and what other muscles do. A concentric action means a muscle shortens while generating force. An eccentric action means it lengthens while producing force, often to control or brake a movement. An isometric action produces tension with little or no change in muscle length. These descriptions apply to the muscle action, not automatically to the entire limb. In a simple standing knee lift, muscles that flex the hip contribute to raising the thigh; the lowering phase may require controlled eccentric activity, although the precise pattern depends on speed and technique. Slow movement is not automatically better or harder in every respect: it changes the time available for correction and the duration of the task.

**A useful comparison:** Think about carrying a full cup of water. Lifting the cup, holding it, and lowering it gently are different tasks even if the starting and ending positions are the same. Dance movement similarly depends on how force is produced and controlled through time.

**Think critically:** A visible pose is evidence of an appearance, not automatic evidence of strength, anatomical structure, safety, or artistic intention. Compare what can be observed with what would require a second camera angle, an explicit movement score, a qualified physical assessment, or a scholarly source.

## Practice with no teacher or barre

**Preparation (3–5 minutes):** Clear a nonslip space, walk or march gently, and make small comfortable movements through the ankles, hips, and shoulders. Do not use a moving treadmill or rolling chair as support. A wall or securely fixed structure can provide light support. If a movement produces pain, dizziness, numbness, or loss of control, stop or choose a seated marking alternative.

**Guided exercise (8–12 minutes):** With feet comfortably apart and one hand on a fixed support if needed, lift the right knee only a few inches on counts 1–2, hold it on 3–4, lower it smoothly on 5–6, and settle on 7–8. Repeat twice. Then use counts 1–4 to lift, count 5 to pause, and counts 6–8 to lower. Keep the range small. Ask whether you can preserve the same trunk position and balance in both timing patterns.

**Original choreography application (5–8 minutes):** In the original Undercurrent motif, the gather on counts 3–4 can be quick or sustained. If the arms stop moving while the body holds its position, that does not mean every muscle becomes inactive; holding still may still require muscular force.

**Repeatability test:** Perform the exercise twice with the same counts. Say which foot supports you, what segment initiates the movement, and one deliberate change between repetitions. If you cannot repeat it comfortably, reduce range and speed before trying again.

## Troubleshooting

| Observation | Possible explanation, not a diagnosis | Self-teaching adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Your torso leans back when the knee rises | The movement may exceed your current comfortable balance or control | Lower the knee, slow the lift, and use a secure fixed support |
| You drop the leg rather than lowering it | The descent may be too quick to control at the chosen range | Use a smaller lift and count the lowering aloud |
| You hold your breath during the pause | You may be treating stillness as full-body rigidity | Reduce effort and breathe normally; do not force breath-holding |

## Knowledge check — try before reading answers

**1. During counts 3–4, a dancer holds the knee in place. Which statement is accurate?**

A. A held knee position proves that the hip muscles have stopped generating tension

B. A stationary knee necessarily means the hip flexors are lengthening

C. A visible hold establishes that no force is required to resist gravity

D. A stationary position can still require muscular tension

**2. The dancer drops the knee quickly on counts 5–6. What is a targeted correction?**

A. Raise the knee higher on counts 3–4 without changing the descent

B. Make the descent smaller and count the lowering phase aloud

C. Keep the same quick drop but lengthen the pause before lowering

D. Add arm movement during counts 5–6 while retaining the uncontrolled drop

**3. What is a concentric muscle action?**

A. A muscle shortens while generating tension

B. A muscle lengthens under tension during the lowering phase

C. A muscle holds approximately the same length while generating tension

D. A joint moves upward whenever any nearby muscle generates force

**4. Why can't an observer infer muscle strength from a held pose alone?**

A. A stationary pose establishes that all active muscles are working at maximal force

B. Matching visible poses guarantees identical loading across dancers

C. Appearance does not establish the force demands or the person's capacity

D. A held position reveals the maximum force the dancer could produce

### Answer key and explanations

**1. D.** Holding a knee in position can require active muscle tension despite no visible movement. A incorrectly equates stillness with no tension, B assumes a particular muscle-length change without evidence, and C overlooks the force needed to resist gravity.

**2. B.** Reduce the descent range and count the lowering phase to address an uncontrolled drop. A changes lift height rather than the descent, C lengthens an unrelated pause, and D adds arm movement while leaving the error intact.

**3. A.** A concentric action occurs when a muscle shortens while generating tension. B describes eccentric action, C describes approximately isometric action, and D confuses muscle contraction type with whole-joint movement direction.

**4. C.** Appearance alone cannot establish force demands, loading, or a person's maximum strength. A and D incorrectly infer maximal force from a hold, while B assumes visually similar poses impose identical demands across different bodies and supports.
