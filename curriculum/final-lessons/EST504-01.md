# EST504 · Lesson 01 — Interpretation: Description Before Meaning

**Dance Criticism & Aesthetics | graduate-level independent study | 50–70 minutes.** Authored learner-facing revision. Scholarly citation audit, expert editorial review, and live-browser testing are outstanding.

## Critical framework

**Outcomes:** Distinguish description, interpretation, and evaluation; cite movement-specific evidence; recognize alternative readings; and critique the work without unsupported assumptions about a performer.

Dance criticism begins by distinguishing what is visible or audible from the meanings a critic proposes. Description might identify a dancer's diagonal reach on counts 5–6, a suspended return, or an averted gaze. Interpretation might suggest longing, refusal, or uncertainty. These are plausible readings, not properties permanently encoded in the gesture. An argument becomes stronger when it identifies multiple supporting details, considers an alternative reading, and names the critic's viewing position. A choreographer's stated intention can be relevant evidence but does not guarantee that audiences perceive the same thing.

## Guided performance-analysis studio

**Preparation:** Clear a safe area and mark a comfortable version of Undercurrent's step–gather–reach–recover motif. If performing is unsuitable, analyze an existing self-created score instead.

**Observe and compare (20–25 minutes):** Perform the Undercurrent motif: step right 1–2, gather 3–4, reach diagonally 5–6, recover 7, stillness 8. Record two versions: A with gaze following the hand and B with gaze directed front. Write three neutral descriptions of each, avoiding emotion words. **For B, identify two distinct observable cues per reading; if both readings rely on the same cues, explicitly explain how the reasoning differs. A score can establish prescribed gaze and counts, but cannot confirm how a recorded performance looked.** Then develop two different interpretations of version B, each supported by two **distinct count-specific cues**. Identify one observation that would weaken each interpretation. **If working from a recording**, cite cues actually visible in the frame; **if no recording is available**, cite prescribed gaze, timing, or pathway details from a count-by-count score and label them **scored choices, not verified performance observations**. Explain the score-only limitation explicitly.

**Revision and ethical check:** If a sentence says 'the dancer feels abandoned,' replace it with a visible cue and qualify the reading: 'the withdrawn gaze may suggest separation.' Do not assume that your own intended emotion is universally perceived.

**Evidence protocol:** Separate your notes into three headings: (1) what can be observed, with counts or timestamps; (2) what you infer and why; and (3) how you evaluate the choice under a stated criterion. A recording does not provide access to private emotions or universal audience reactions.

## Knowledge check

**1. Which is a descriptive statement?**

A. The diagonal reach demonstrates that every viewer experiences grief

B. The right arm reaches diagonally on counts 5–6

C. The arm pathway reveals that the performer privately regrets leaving

D. The reach's duration guarantees sadness in the audience

**2. What makes an interpretation more defensible?**

A. Presenting the choreographer's stated intention as the only possible reading

B. Choosing the most dramatic interpretation without examining the movement evidence

C. Connecting it to observable cues and alternatives

D. Inferring the performer's private thoughts from one gesture alone

**3. Which statement correctly treats intention?**

A. Choreographer intention is relevant but does not determine every audience reading

B. The choreographer's intention is irrelevant even when a documented program note exists

C. A documented intention establishes the emotional response of every viewer

D. The performer's movement alone proves their exact intention without other sources

**4. What should a critic do with a competing reading?**

A. Dismiss a competing reading because it conflicts with the thesis

B. Reject the alternative because the choreographer intended something different

C. Summarize the alternative without considering its supporting observations

D. Consider what observations support or weaken it

### Answer key and rationale

**1. B.** The right-arm direction and counts 5–6 are directly inspectable in a score or suitable recording. Claims about every viewer's grief (A), private regret (C), or guaranteed sadness (D) infer psychological states that the movement cannot establish.

**2. C.** A defensible interpretation links observable movement details to a proposed meaning and considers other readings. Intention alone cannot dictate every response (A), drama without evidence is unsupported (B), and one gesture cannot reveal private thoughts (D).

**3. A.** Documented choreographer intention can inform an interpretation but does not predetermine audience reception. Dismissing documented intention entirely (B), asserting universal reception (C), or inferring exact intention from movement alone (D) all exceed the available evidence.

**4. D.** A critic should compare the observations supporting and challenging a competing interpretation. Rejecting a reading merely because it differs from the thesis (A) or intention (B), or listing it without testing evidence (C), does not critically evaluate it.

**Self-correction:** For missed items, rewrite one unsupported assertion as a situated, evidence-supported claim. These questions are instructional, not a validated critical-writing examination.

## Independent critical assignment

Produce a 300–450-word close reading of the two taught eight-count Undercurrent versions: A follows the hand with the gaze; B faces front. Include at least three neutral, count-specific observations for each, two competing interpretations of B each tied to two visible cues, and one observation that would weaken each interpretation. Separate description, interpretation and evaluation under explicit headings. State whether evidence comes from a recording or an authored score; do not claim to know a performer's private feelings.

**Worked example:** “Both versions reach diagonally on counts 5–6. In A my gaze follows the hand, while in B I keep it forward. I could read B as addressing an audience or as withholding attention from the reach; neither reading is a visible fact. A clearer change in head direction would help test which cues I am relying on.”

**Rubric:** Accurate neutral description with counts 35%; evidence-linked competing interpretations 30%; distinction between observation and evaluation 20%; recording/source limitations and ethical language 15%.
