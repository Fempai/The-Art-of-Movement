# STG101 · Lesson 03 — Trunk Control: Breathing, Bracing, and Balance

**Independent dance conditioning | 35–50 minutes.** This lesson is an authored learner-facing revision. Final citation, professional safety, and live-browser audits remain outstanding. Beginners should choose comfortable loads and stop for pain, dizziness, chest discomfort, or unusual shortness of breath. Seek qualified medical advice when symptoms warrant it.

## Learn: movement and training principle

The trunk transfers forces among the arms, pelvis, and legs during movement. 'Core' refers to coordinated muscular control rather than one isolated abdominal muscle or a particular appearance. Bracing is one strategy for creating trunk stiffness under load, but dance also requires controlled rotation and yielding. The aim in this lesson is to manage small weight shifts while breathing normally, not to hold the abdomen rigid throughout an entire phrase. Balance is task-specific: stability in a stationary stance does not prove readiness for turns or aerial skills.

**Training principle:** Repetition quality and repeatable conditions matter more than a dramatic single attempt. A self-directed website cannot assess individual injury risk, prescribe rehabilitation, or certify technique from a self-reported score.

## Guided conditioning lab

**Warm-up and practice:** Stand near a fixed support with feet hip-width apart, or sit securely. Place hands on lower ribs and take three comfortable breaths without forcing depth. **One eight-count cycle consists of four phases, not eight separate shifts:** shift weight slightly right over counts 1–2, return to center on 3–4, shift left on 5–6, and return to center on 7–8. Count support touches across the **eight-count cycle** (up to eight count positions), not as 'eight shifts.' For a seated version, use a stable nonrolling chair with feet supported and shift sitting pressure slightly right and left on the same counts; this is a seated coordination task, not a standing-balance measurement. Repeat with arms reaching forward on 3–4 while maintaining a comfortable upright position. For a second drill, hold a light object close to the chest, turn the torso a few degrees right and left without twisting knees, and return to center. Do two sets of four gentle turns. Avoid heavy loading or breath-holding.

**Observe and troubleshoot:** Notice whether you can maintain a steady count and breathe normally as the arms move. A useful record says 'I needed to touch the support on two of eight shifts,' not 'my core is weak.' If dizziness, pain, or numbness occurs, stop and seek appropriate professional advice if symptoms persist.

**Transfer to dance:** Perform one eight-count Undercurrent step–gather–reach–recover motif in a comfortable range. Ask whether your breathing, posture, and transitions remain controllable. Conditioning supports the phrase; the phrase is not a test of maximal strength.

**Ready for progression?** You can complete the chosen version without pain, loss of control, or compensatory rushing, and can explain exactly which training variable you would change. Do not progress solely because a numerical repetition target was met.

## Knowledge check

**1. What is the main goal of this trunk-control lesson?**

A. Holding a fixed trunk position until fatigue prevents controlled breathing

B. Making the largest possible weight shift regardless of support control

C. Controlled shifts with comfortable breathing

D. Keeping the torso rigid while ignoring changes in the support foot

**2. Which observation is measurable?**

A. The dancer's perceived confidence without counting support changes

B. Two support touches in eight shifts

C. The dancer's reported effort without recording any observable event

D. The dancer's intended stability without noting whether support was used

**3. Why practice with a stable support nearby?**

A. To reduce consequences of a balance error

B. To eliminate the need to reduce range when balance is inconsistent

C. To ensure that no weight shifts occur during the exercise

D. To make it possible to use a larger unsupported range immediately

**4. Which claim is too broad?**

A. Balance is task-specific

B. Breathing can continue during light practice

C. Range can be reduced

D. Completing a still standing balance test establishes readiness for rapid turns in every direction

### Answer key with explanations

**1. C.** The goal is controlled weight shifts with comfortable breathing. A continues until breathing deteriorates, B prioritizes maximum range without support control, and D imposes rigidity while ignoring changing support.

**2. B.** Two support touches during eight shifts is a directly countable event. A, C, and D rely on confidence, effort, or intended stability without recording an observable support event.

**3. A.** A stable rail or other secure support can reduce the consequences of a balance error. B falsely removes the need to scale range, C contradicts the goal of controlled shifts, and D encourages immediate unsupported progression.

**4. D.** A still-standing test does not establish readiness for rapid multidirectional turns, which impose different timing and balance demands. A, B, and C are limited, task-appropriate statements rather than universal readiness claims.

**Self-review:** Correct answers test the stated training distinctions. For a missed question, explain why the chosen distractor is incorrect and repeat the relevant demonstration with a comfortable modification. These questions are not a validated health or fitness test.

## Independent assignment

For Lesson 03, submit a trunk-control baseline: support used, eight-count right/left shifts, comfortable breathing, number of support touches **during each eight-count cycle** (record the count positions), and the result of a second eight-count cycle after a small-range modification; note whether the light-object rotation was attempted and why.

**Worked example:** “During one eight-count cycle of right–center–left–center shifts I touched the rail twice and continued breathing comfortably. I reduced the distance of the left shift and needed one support touch on the next eight-count attempt. I recorded the support and range change rather than interpreting the result as a diagnosis of core weakness.”
