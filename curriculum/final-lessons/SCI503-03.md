# SCI503 · Lesson 03 — Feedback: Compare, Correct, and Retest

**Motor Learning & Dance Science | graduate-level independent study | 45–60 minutes.** Learner-facing revision; source audit, expert review, and browser testing remain outstanding. Do not intentionally train to exhaustion or push through pain.

## Lecture: explain the learning mechanism

**Learning outcomes:** Distinguish observed performance from inferred learning, apply one defined practice method, collect repeatable movement observations, and identify what the data cannot prove.

Feedback can come from intrinsic information, such as sensing a weight shift, or from augmented sources, such as a count sheet, video, or another person's comments. Knowledge of results concerns the outcome (for example, whether the reach arrived by count 6); knowledge of performance concerns how the movement unfolded (for example, whether the shoulder elevated). For independent learners, self-video is useful for visible timing and pathways but cannot establish hidden joint forces or diagnose injury. Too much correction on every attempt can distract from learning, so this lesson uses a focused observation, one adjustment, and a later unprompted retest.

## Guided movement-science laboratory

**Prepare:** Warm up with gentle walking or seated weight shifts. Choose a safe, small-range version of the eight-count Undercurrent step–gather–reach–recover motif.

**Protocol (15–20 minutes):** Choose one measurable target: reach begins on count 5 and returns on count 7. Perform three trials from memory. After each, use a fixed-angle recording or immediately mark your count sheet. Write the observed onset and return count, then identify one specific error pattern. If onset is early, rehearse only gather on 3–4 and reach on 5–6 twice at half speed. Then perform two whole-phrase trials without looking at feedback until both are complete. Compare counts; note any uncertainty or poor camera visibility.

**Troubleshooting and limitations:** If the recording misses the hands, reposition the camera before drawing conclusions. If you are uncertain about a joint position, do not invent a biomechanical diagnosis; seek qualified feedback when needed. If the correction creates tension, reduce range.

**Research integrity check:** Record the planned trial count, actual trial count, condition, prompts, and deviations. Do not change scoring definitions after seeing outcomes without documenting the change. A solo log can help direct practice but cannot establish general causal effects.

## Knowledge check

**1. Which is knowledge of results?**

A. The reach arrived by count 6

B. The shoulder elevated during the reach, describing execution rather than target achievement

C. The performer intended to convey longing, rather than reporting a measured result

D. The dancer reached with a raised shoulder, without reporting whether the timing target was met

**2. Which is knowledge of performance?**

A. The reach finished on count 6, reporting outcome rather than execution

B. The shoulder elevated as the arm traveled

C. The dancer met the final count target without describing the movement pathway

D. The reach arrived on the prescribed beat, describing accuracy rather than movement mechanics

**3. What is a suitable feedback correction?**

A. Change reach height and tempo together, making the timing error harder to isolate

B. Diagnose a shoulder injury from the timing error without relevant evidence

C. Isolate the early onset and retest the full phrase

D. Increase reach distance without checking whether the early onset changes

**4. What can a fixed-angle video not establish?**

A. The hand's visible position when the recording clearly includes it

B. The beat on which a clearly visible reach begins

C. Whether the dancer remains in the fixed camera's frame

D. Exact hidden joint forces or a medical diagnosis

### Answer key and explanation

**1. A.** Saying the reach arrived by count 6 reports the timing outcome against a target: knowledge of results. Shoulder elevation (B), artistic intention (C), and a raised-shoulder execution description without outcome timing (D) do not report target achievement.

**2. B.** Shoulder elevation during the arm pathway describes movement execution, which is knowledge of performance. Finishing on count 6 (A), meeting a final-count target (C), and arriving on the prescribed beat (D) report results rather than execution mechanics.

**3. C.** Isolate the early reach onset, rehearse a corrected entry, and retest the phrase against the same timing target. Changing tempo and height together (A), inferring an unrelated cause without evidence (B), or altering distance without testing onset (D) does not address the measured error.

**4. D.** Fixed-angle video may document visible hand position (A), the onset beat of an observable reach (B), and whether the performer remains in frame (C). It cannot directly measure hidden joint forces or establish a medical diagnosis without other methods and qualified assessment.

**Self-review:** Explain why any missed alternative confuses outcome with process, immediate performance with learning, or observation with causal inference. These are original teaching questions, not validated cognitive or clinical assessments.

## Independent assignment

Submit a dated protocol, a trial log, and a 250–350-word analysis describing the method, results, one competing explanation, and one justified change to future practice. **Lesson 04 capstone:** a **learning study** with baseline plus planned day-1, day-3, and day-7 checks; a completed report must wait until those sessions actually occur. Include a graph or table, deviations, and a limitations paragraph.

**Worked example:** “My reach began on count 4 in trial 1 and count 5 in trials 2–3. I isolated the gather–reach boundary and then retested. This is an observed short-term improvement in execution; it does not prove permanent learning or identify the cause.”
