# PRO701 · Lesson 02 — Rehearsal Architecture: Chunking, Cues, and Run-Throughs

**Professional Repertory Laboratory | independent performance study | 50–70 minutes.** Learner-facing revision; scholarly verification, expert adjudication, and live browser testing remain pending.

## Performance principle

**Objectives:** Define a reproducible performance criterion, apply a deliberate rehearsal method, compare observed execution with intention, and revise safely.

A productive rehearsal separates acquisition, targeted correction, integration, and performance simulation. Chunking divides a longer phrase into meaningful units, while cueing identifies the information needed to start each unit. Repeated full run-throughs without diagnosis may reinforce errors and increase fatigue. A rehearsal plan should specify what to practice, how to observe it, when to rest, and what evidence will determine whether to advance. For solo dancers, a count sheet and fixed-camera recording can provide external cues, though they do not replace qualified feedback for complex technique.

## Guided repertory laboratory

**Prepare (5–8 minutes):** Clear a nonslip area. Warm up with easy steps, gentle reaches, and comfortable changes of direction. No unsupervised aerials, lifts, or extreme ranges are required.

**Rehearse (20–30 minutes):** Divide the 32-count Undercurrent score into A (1–8), B (9–16), A′ (17–24), and C (25–32). Write a one-word cue for each: 'arrive,' 'redirect,' 'sustain,' 'resolve.' Rehearse each chunk once at half speed and once at comfortable speed. Connect A→B and A′→C separately, checking whether each transition uses the scored counts. Rest for one minute, then perform a single 32-count run without looking at the score. Mark which chunk starts required prompting. Rehearse only that entry twice before a final run; stop earlier if movement quality declines.

**Troubleshooting:** If you forget a chunk, rehearse its entry cue and first two counts rather than restarting the whole dance repeatedly. If the transition needs an extra step, update the score or simplify the pathway. A shorter accurate rehearsal is preferable to repeated deteriorating runs.

**Accessible modification:** If a standing run is not suitable, rehearse the A–B–A′–C entries seated using supported weight shifts and arm gestures. Keep the same one-word entry cues and count boundaries, but mark travel and foot-placement criteria as adapted or not assessed. Compare seated trials with other seated trials rather than claiming equivalence to full-body repertory.

**Independent observation:** Keep an annotated score, recording the planned count, what actually occurred, the viewing or recording limitations, and a single correction. When using video, preserve the camera position for comparisons.

## Knowledge check

**1. What is chunking?**

A. Repeating the full phrase faster so it can be memorized in fewer attempts

B. Grouping a long phrase into manageable units

C. Practicing only isolated poses without linking the phrase boundaries

D. Performing the complete phrase continuously without dividing it into sections

**2. What is the purpose of a cue word?**

A. To substitute a verbal label for practicing the transition it names

B. To guarantee that remembering the word ensures the section is performed correctly

C. To help initiate a specific section

D. To eliminate the need to check support-foot changes at section entries

**3. Which rehearsal sequence is most purposeful?**

A. Chunk, connect, run, diagnose, revise

B. Repeat the full phrase after each error without identifying the failed section

C. Alter the movement order after each run so no stable target is maintained

D. Practice the final section alone while leaving earlier entry errors unexamined

**4. What should you do if run quality deteriorates?**

A. Add more transitions while the current ones are becoming less controlled

B. Raise tempo to finish the planned number of runs despite declining control

C. Continue the same workload because the earlier runs were accurate

D. Rest or end the session

### Answers and explanations

**1. B.** Chunking groups a longer phrase into smaller units that can be rehearsed and reconnected. Accelerating the whole phrase (A), isolating poses without transitions (C), and never dividing the sequence (D) do not implement the taught chunking procedure.

**2. C.** A cue word helps initiate a particular section and can support memory at a transition. It cannot replace movement practice (A), guarantee accurate execution (B), or remove the need to check support-foot changes (D).

**3. A.** Chunk, connect, run, diagnose, and revise creates a structured loop from rehearsal to evidence-based correction. Undiagnosed repetition (B), continually changing the target (C), and neglecting earlier entry errors (D) leave important mistakes unresolved.

**4. D.** Resting or ending the session is appropriate when control deteriorates, so later practice can resume under manageable conditions. Adding transitions (A), raising tempo (B), or ignoring declining quality (C) increases demands without correcting the problem.

**Self-correction:** For missed answers, demonstrate the corrected concept in a four- or eight-count example. This knowledge check is not a validated measure of professional performance ability.

## Assignment and assessment

Submit a 32-count A (1–8), B (9–16), A′ (17–24), C (25–32) score with one-word entry cues (‘arrive,’ ‘redirect,’ ‘sustain,’ ‘resolve’). Log each chunk at half speed and comfortable speed, A→B and A′→C transition checks, one unprompted run after rest, two targeted repetitions of the weakest entry, and one final run if safe. Mark any prompted starts, extra steps, and changes in quality. Write a 250–350-word reflection distinguishing targeted correction from repeated undiagnosed run-throughs.

**Worked example:** “My first 32-count run needed a verbal cue at A′ count 17. I rehearsed the A′ entry and first two counts twice, then repeated the full score without reading it. I recorded whether the entry was unprompted; I did not assume that remembering the cue guaranteed correct foot support.”

**Rubric:** Accurate four-chunk score and entry cues 30%; transparent rehearsal sequence and observations 30%; targeted transition correction 25%; safe workload and reflection 15%.
