# RES501 · Lesson 03 — Observation: Build a Reliable Movement-Event Log

**Graduate-level dance research methods | 50–70 minutes | independent study.** This learner-facing revision has not completed scholarly passage verification, expert review, or browser acceptance testing.

## Lecture: research reasoning

**Outcomes:** Define the method in this lesson, use it in a small inquiry, separate evidence from interpretation, and document the limits of any conclusion.

Observation converts a complex performance into records that can be inspected. A coding scheme defines each event before collection. For example, define 'reach onset' as the first frame in which the right hand begins traveling outward after the gather, and 'successful transition' as a phrase boundary with no unscored corrective step. Definitions reduce ambiguity but do not eliminate judgment. A solo observer may know which performance was intended to be slow, and that knowledge can bias coding. Rewatching a clip can improve precision but also create false certainty if camera framing is poor. Record missing or ambiguous events explicitly rather than filling gaps.

## Guided research laboratory

**Preparation (5 minutes):** Create a dated research notebook. State the research question before gathering observations. If movement is involved, warm up gently, use a safe space, and choose comfortable small-range movements.

**Apply the method (20–25 minutes):** Use a fixed camera position or a paper log if recording is unavailable. Perform the 8-count motif six times at a comfortable pace. Create columns for trial number, expected onset count (5), observed onset count, transition correction (yes/no), visibility (clear/unclear), and note. Write your operational definitions at the top. Code the six trials, then wait briefly and recode two without consulting the original entries. Compare the codes and explain discrepancies. Do not calculate a meaningful reliability coefficient from two informal recodes.

**Troubleshoot your reasoning:** If a hand leaves the frame, mark onset 'not observable' rather than guessing. If the performer needs a stabilizing step, record it as a correction rather than concealing it. Avoid interpreting a delayed reach as a psychological trait.

**Connect to Undercurrent:** Treat the original step–gather–reach–recover phrase as a bounded study object. Describe its count score, the exact feature under investigation, and whether your evidence comes from rehearsal, video, testimony, or published research. Do not call a personal impression a universal result.

## Knowledge check

**1. What is an operational definition?**

A. A claim that a reach looked confident without defining a visible event

B. A rule specifying how an event will be observed or recorded

C. A broad research topic without a rule for marking each trial

D. A count-sheet heading without a criterion for recording onset

**2. What should you record when the hand is outside the camera frame?**

A. The count inferred from the previous visible arm position

B. The count shown in the choreography score even though the hand was not visible

C. Not observable

D. The onset count recorded in the nearest fully visible trial

**3. Why recode two trials later?**

A. To inspect whether coding decisions are consistent

B. To establish that the same person would code all future trials identically

C. To remove all interpretation differences simply by repeating the coding

D. To turn a single-performer dataset into a representative sample

**4. Which statement overclaims the pilot?**

A. The visible onset count was documented for each trial in which the reach could be observed

B. Four visible onsets were recorded under the stated camera setup

C. Two trials could not be coded unambiguously because the hand was outside the frame

D. These six trials establish how all dancers move

### Answer key and explanations

**1. B.** An operational definition specifies the visible event and the rule for recording it, so the same procedure can be repeated. A subjective impression (A), broad topic (C), or unlabeled heading without an onset criterion (D) is not a measurement rule.

**2. C.** Mark the onset not observable when the hand is outside the frame, and note the recording limitation. Guessing from an earlier position (A), copying the intended score (B), or borrowing a nearby trial's count (D) substitutes inference for observation.

**3. A.** Recoding selected trials after a delay checks whether the same operational rule produces consistent decisions. It cannot guarantee future agreement (B), eliminate every interpretive difference (C), or make a one-person sample representative (D).

**4. D.** Six trials from one performer cannot establish how all dancers move; that conclusion exceeds the sampling design. Reporting visible onset counts (A), the four observed events (B), and the two uncodable trials (C) describes the actual evidence without generalizing beyond it.

**Feedback cycle:** Explain why each missed alternative overreaches or misidentifies evidence. Then revise one claim in your notebook. These original questions check concepts, not graduate credit or standardized research competence.

## Independent assignment

Submit a research artifact specific to this lesson (question matrix, evidence matrix, observation log, or proposal) and a 250–350-word methodological reflection. State at least two limitations and one change you would make before repeating the inquiry. **Lesson 04 capstone:** a 600–800-word **research proposal** with a question, sources, procedure, operational definitions, analysis plan, reflexivity, ethics, and limits.

**Worked example:** “I defined reach onset as the first visible outward hand motion after the gather. In trial 2, the hand was outside the frame, so onset was coded 'not observable.' I will widen the camera framing before collecting more trials. This adjustment improves documentation; it does not establish a causal relationship.”

**Rubric:** Question and method alignment 25%; evidence quality and traceability 30%; transparent limitations and reflexivity 25%; organization and justified next steps 20%. Do not award credit for unsupported causal claims or fabricated source details.
