# PRO702 · Lesson 01 — Lighting: Visibility, Contrast, and Cue Design

**Stagecraft & Artistic Direction | independent production study | 50–70 minutes.** Authored learner-facing revision. Scholarly passage verification, expert production review, and live browser testing remain pending.

## Production principle

**Outcomes:** Explain a stagecraft variable, plan it with a diagram or cue sheet, test an observable effect safely, and document a revision.

Lighting design makes performance visible and shapes visual emphasis through intensity, direction, color, and timing. For a solo home rehearsal, use existing room lights and safe positioning rather than installing theatrical fixtures. Backlighting can create a silhouette but may obscure facial detail; front lighting can make hands visible but create glare or flatten depth. A lighting cue specifies an observable change and a trigger, not just a mood adjective. Document the intended effect and test whether the movement remains visible from the recording position. Never handle energized fixtures, overload outlets, cover lamps with fabric, or rehearse in darkness.

## Guided production laboratory

**Prepare:** Use a level, clear, permitted rehearsal area. Keep exits unobstructed. Do not install lighting equipment, alter wiring, climb rigging, or use hazardous props.

**Plan and test (20–30 minutes):** Set a phone or camera at a stable, permitted position. Record the Undercurrent eight-count motif with ordinary overhead lighting, then with a safe existing side lamp positioned away from travel routes. Keep camera exposure settings consistent if possible; if automatic exposure changes, note that limitation. Observe whether the reach on counts 5–6 and recovery on 7 remain visible. Draft cue LQ1: 'Before count 1: steady room light; objective: full-body visibility.' Draft cue LQ2 as a proposed change at count 17, without actually switching lights during movement unless another authorized person safely controls the switch. Add a fallback: steady light for the entire piece.

**Troubleshooting and safety:** If the hand becomes invisible against a bright window, change dancer facing or camera angle. If a cable crosses the performance area, remove the lamp. Do not infer that a dramatic shadow is necessarily a better design.

**Undercurrent application:** Link each staging choice to the original step–gather–reach–recover score. Record what the audience might be able to see, but distinguish a visibility prediction from tested reception.

## Knowledge check

**1. What is a useful lighting cue?**

A. A note to increase intensity without specifying when the change occurs

B. A timed or triggered change with a defined visual objective

C. A description of the desired mood without identifying the lighting state

D. A lighting color named without a trigger or visual purpose

**2. What might strong backlighting obscure?**

A. The exact timing of the musical pulse even though the sound is unchanged

B. Facial and front-body detail

C. The measured size of the stage floor in the technical plan

D. The direction of travel recorded in the choreography score

**3. What is the safest home-lighting approach?**

A. Reposition a ceiling-mounted fixture without trained assistance

B. Drape fabric over a warm lamp to soften the light

C. Use existing lights without creating trip hazards

D. Turn off all pathway lights to create a stronger silhouette

**4. What should a designer do when camera exposure changes between takes?**

A. Treat the camera's automatic exposure as proof that the stage light changed

B. Compare the two images as though their exposure settings were matched

C. Attribute a change in visible detail to the dancer without checking exposure

D. Document the exposure difference as a comparison limit

### Correct answers and explanations

**1. B.** A usable lighting cue specifies the intended visual change, when it occurs, and why it matters. An untimed intensity note (A), mood without a lighting state (C), and color without a trigger or objective (D) cannot reliably guide a repeatable cue.

**2. B.** Strong backlighting can silhouette a performer and obscure facial or front-body detail. It does not change the audible pulse (A), the dimensions of a documented stage plan (C), or the travel direction written in a score (D).

**3. C.** Existing, stable lights with clear walking routes avoid unnecessary electrical and trip risks. Altering ceiling fixtures without assistance (A), covering warm lamps with fabric (B), and removing pathway lighting (D) introduce hazards unrelated to the teaching objective.

**4. D.** Record exposure differences before comparing apparent brightness or visible detail across takes. Automatic camera exposure does not prove a stage-light change (A), make mismatched images equivalent (B), or establish a change in the dancer's execution (C).

**Self-review:** For any missed item, revise the relevant blocking, lighting, or risk-control entry. These original questions check planning concepts, not technical certification.

## Independent assignment

Submit a two-state lighting cue sheet for the eight-count Undercurrent motif using only existing safe room lighting (ordinary overhead and stable side lamp). Identify each state, cue trigger, camera position, and visibility of the hands on counts 5–6. Record both tests, noting exposure changes, glare, shadows and one safe adjustment; include a 250–350-word rationale distinguishing planned illumination from what the camera actually captured. No electrical installation or live operator is required.

**Worked example:** “My side lamp improved visibility of the reach on counts 5–6, but the phone changed exposure between takes. I noted that limitation before changing the cue sheet. I did not infer a brighter physical light solely from the camera image.”

**Rubric:** Specific cue states and triggers 30%; comparable observation and exposure limits 30%; electrical and pathway safety 25%; justified artistic revision 15%.
