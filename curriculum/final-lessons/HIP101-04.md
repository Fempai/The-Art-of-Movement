# HIP101 · Lesson 04 — Hip-Hop Cultural Foundations: Place, People, and Respectful Practice

**Learner-facing draft | 40–55 minutes.** The website is the instructor for this introductory lesson. This is not a certification of technique or an endorsement of unsupported cultural attributions. Scholarly metadata and deep links still require independent verification.

## Learn the idea

Hip-hop culture is associated with Black and Latino youth communities in the Bronx and New York City in the 1970s, amid specific social, economic, and urban conditions. It is often discussed through interconnected practices such as DJing, MCing, breaking, and graffiti, but the histories of these practices are complex and should not be compressed into a single inventor or a single birthday. Breaking developed in particular social contexts; it is not interchangeable with every street dance or with studio choreography marketed as 'hip-hop.' Scholarship by Jeff Chang, Tricia Rose, and Joseph Schloss offers different but complementary perspectives on history, music, cultural politics, and practitioners' accounts. Respectful study means identifying whose practice you are learning, using specific names when known, avoiding claims of ownership, and recognizing that movement knowledge is transmitted through communities as well as texts. The contemporary dance-studio category 'hip-hop' can include movements from distinct traditions; a beginner must learn to distinguish rather than flatten them.

**Vocabulary in practice:** A *count* is a numbered timing reference, a *pulse* is a recurring temporal reference, an *accent* is a point of emphasis, and a *weight shift* is a change in how your body is supported. These are working descriptions for the exercises below, not universal definitions of a dance tradition.

## Follow-along practice

**Prepare (4 minutes):** Clear a nonslip space. March gently, shift weight between feet, and move shoulders in a comfortable range. Keep knees soft; avoid forced twists, maximal knee bends, and unstable furniture. Stop for pain, dizziness, numbness, or repeated loss of control.

**Practice (10–15 minutes):** Choose a comfortable groove from Lesson 01. Counts **1** shift pressure right with a shallow pulse, **2** return toward center, **3** shift pressure left with a shallow pulse, and **4** return center, without adding a named historical step. For the answer, **5** begin a small right-arm gesture, **6** complete it, **7** turn the head or upper torso slightly toward a comfortable new facing **without twisting planted knees or forcing a foot pivot**, and **8** settle with both feet supported. A change in gaze alone is acceptable when turning is uncomfortable. Repeat and write down which elements are original to you. Do not label your newly invented movement as a historically authenticated breaking step. If studying a named social dance from a video later, record the dancer, community, place, and source where those facts are actually documented.

**Modification and self-check:** If weight shifts are uncomfortable or standing support is limited, sit on a stable nonrolling chair with both feet supported: shift sitting pressure right on 1, center on 2, left on 3, center on 4; gesture with the right arm on 5–6, turn gaze slightly on 7, and settle on 8. This substitutes gaze for a full-body facing change. For a quieter version, mark the groove at half speed without bouncing. Check whether the intended accents still occur on the written counts; if not, reduce the movement size before increasing speed. These are practice alternatives, not claims that a seated groove reproduces the original cultural form.

**What to notice:** A historical claim such as 'this movement originated in a specific crew in a particular year' requires evidence. A personal statement such as 'I used a downward pulse on counts 2 and 4' can be supported by your own movement score. Do not substitute one for the other.

**Apply to Undercurrent (5–8 minutes):** Begin with the original step–gather–reach–recover phrase. Keep the eight-count framework, but replace one accent or weight transfer with today's concept. Perform your new version twice. Write the counts and explain what you intentionally changed. An original exercise is not a historical claim.

## Fix common difficulties

| Observation | Possible explanation | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| You treat breaking, popping, locking, and all social dances as synonyms | Distinct practices have different histories and communities | Use precise terminology and research each tradition separately |
| You cannot identify the origin of a step | The source may not document its lineage | Mark origin as unknown instead of inventing attribution |
| You confuse a classroom exercise with an authentic historical dance | An original teaching drill is not necessarily a community practice | Label the exercise original and cite historical sources only for supported context |

**Progression checkpoint:** You can maintain an eight-count pulse, reproduce the phrase twice in a comfortable range, explain the difference between observation and interpretation, and avoid presenting a personal invention as an attributed historical step. A written score cannot verify expert performance quality.

## Knowledge check

**1. Which setting is central to accounts of early hip-hop culture?**

A. A single commercial studio where every early hip-hop practice was formally standardized

B. Black and Latino communities in 1970s New York City, especially the Bronx

C. A national televised dance contest that predates the Bronx community scene

D. A single choreographer whose work fully explains all early hip-hop practices

**2. Why should breaking not be used as a synonym for every hip-hop-related dance?**

A. Breaking describes every movement performed to hip-hop music, regardless of technique

B. The term breaking refers only to modern studio choreography, not a community practice

C. Different styles and practices have distinct histories and techniques

D. The same music can be used for different dances, so their movement histories must be identical

**3. What should you do when a video's movement origin is uncertain?**

A. Record the uncertainty and research before attributing it

B. Label the movement traditional based only on how often it appears online

C. Attribute it to the video's uploader without evidence they originated the movement

D. Use the broad term hip-hop as proof of a specific regional origin

**4. Which is a defensible statement about your original phrase?**

A. The rightward shift on count one was historically used by every early Bronx dancer

B. My original eight-count phrase accurately reproduces a named historical dance without checking sources

C. A video of my phrase demonstrates its cultural lineage simply because the music is hip-hop

D. I used a rightward weight shift on count one

### Answers and explanations

**1. B.** Accounts of early hip-hop culture center Black and Latino communities in 1970s New York City, particularly the Bronx. A invents a single standardized studio, C substitutes a supposed earlier national contest, and D wrongly reduces a community history to one choreographer.

**2. C.** Breaking is a distinct practice with its own history; hip-hop-related dances should not all be collapsed into one label. A treats music as proof of style, B erases breaking's community roots, and D assumes sharing music means sharing identical movement histories.

**3. A.** Record the uncertainty and seek reliable attribution evidence before assigning a specific origin. B treats online repetition as authentication, C confuses uploader with creator, and D assumes an umbrella genre label proves regional provenance.

**4. D.** A first-person score can document the rightward shift performed on count one. A generalizes to every early Bronx dancer, B asserts historical reproduction without evidence, and C treats music choice as proof of cultural lineage.

## Independent assignment

Produce a two-phrase, sixteen-count original groove and attribution study. In phrase A (counts 1–8), use a comfortable weight-shift groove for 1–4 and an answering arm gesture with a modest facing change for 5–8. In phrase B (counts 9–16), repeat with one clearly documented change of accent or facing. Identify which decisions are your own, and do not describe either phrase as an authenticated historical step without appropriate evidence. Provide (1) a count-by-count movement score, (2) 250–350 words explaining your rhythmic choices, (3) one precise cultural or historical claim supported by an appropriate source with a specific page, chapter, or verifiable passage, and (4) one claim you deliberately avoid making because the evidence is insufficient. Explicitly distinguish the historical claim from your invented phrase; if the available source does not substantiate the proposed claim, replace it with a narrower claim that it does support.

**Worked example:** “I created a small eight-count groove using a weight shift on counts 1–4 and an answering arm gesture on 5–8. My count sheet establishes what I performed, not where a historical step originated. I would need documented practitioner or archival evidence before attributing a named move to a particular community or crew.”

**Rubric:** Rhythmic description 25%; clear and repeatable movement design 25%; cultural precision and attribution 25%; evidence, limitations, and reflection 25%. Do not grade a learner's cultural authenticity from their body or appearance.

**[2]** Rose, T. (1994). Black Noise: Rap Music and Black Culture in Contemporary America. Wesleyan University Press. https://www.wesleyan.edu/wespress/ — scholarly analysis of rap and cultural politics; publisher homepage is a discovery link, not a verified edition-specific page.

**[3]** Schloss, J. G. (2009). Foundation: B-Boys, B-Girls and Hip-Hop Culture in New York. Oxford University Press. https://global.oup.com/academic/ — ethnographic scholarship on breaking and practitioners; publisher homepage is a discovery link, not a verified book-specific URL.
