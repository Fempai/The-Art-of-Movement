# LAB101 · Lesson 03 — Movement Memory: Chunking, Retrieval, and Correction

**Original repertory | 40–55 minutes | independent learner.** This is an authored lesson revision, not a certified professional performance evaluation. Source auditing and browser testing remain pending.

## Objective and explanation

Retrieve the connected A–B sixteen-count sequence without reading the score, compare an immediate and a post-break attempt, and document a specific count-level correction and the result of a second unaided retrieval.

Movement memory improves when a dancer can retrieve a sequence rather than only imitate it while reading. **Chunking** groups related actions into manageable units; **retrieval practice** means trying to recall the sequence before looking at the score; **feedback** compares the attempt with the target. For a solo learner, video can reveal count errors, but it cannot replace expert judgment on advanced technique. Use a planned cycle: study → mark → retrieve → check → correct → retrieve again. A successful attempt is not one lucky run; it is repeatability across trials and after a short break.

## Follow-along studio

**Prepare (5 minutes):** Clear a nonslip area. Warm up with gentle weight shifts, comfortable shoulder circles, and small arm reaches. Keep the movement within your own pain-free range. Use seated marking when balance or standing is uncomfortable; do not attempt falls, inversions, or unsupported acrobatics.

**Guided rehearsal (15 minutes):** Study phrase A from Lesson 01 for one minute. Hide the score and perform it from memory. Check counts 1–2, 3–4, 5–6, and 7–8 separately, marking each correct or needing revision. Next add phrase B: 9 step left, 10 settle, 11–12 sweep arms horizontally, 13–14 rotate the torso a small comfortable diagonal, 15 lower arms, 16 return front. Retrieve A and B together without reading. Rest for two minutes, then retrieve again. If the transition fails, practice only counts 7–10 before repeating the full sequence. **Seated option:** Use a stable nonrolling chair with both feet supported. For A, mark count 1 with a small rightward seated-pressure shift, 2 settle, 3–4 gather, 5–6 reach right, 7 return, 8 recover. For B, mark 9 with a small leftward seated-pressure shift, 10 settle, 11–12 sweep arms horizontally, 13–14 turn the gaze to a comfortable diagonal while the torso stays front, 15 lower arms, 16 return gaze front. Retrieve the same 16 counts without reading the score; label seated pressure and gaze substitutions as adaptations, **not** evidence of standing steps or turns. Check the 8→9 boundary for a supported neutral-to-left shift without adding a count.

**Solo feedback (5 minutes):** If you remember arms but forget footwork, speak the support foot on counts 1, 2, 9, and 10. If you can only perform while looking at the page, shorten the chunk to four counts and rebuild. If you recall the same mistake three times, check the score before repeating rather than reinforcing the error.

**Memory and expressive check:** First perform while reading the score, then from memory, then compare with the score. Write two factual observations (for example, 'I reached on count 6 rather than 5') and one artistic intention (for example, 'I wanted the delay to suggest anticipation'). Do not confuse the two.

## Knowledge check

**1. What is retrieval practice?**

A. Looking at the score while copying each movement immediately

B. Attempting recall without looking at the score

C. Performing the phrase faster while keeping the score visible

D. Watching the recording again without attempting the phrase independently

**2. What is chunking?**

A. Dividing a sequence into manageable units

B. Practicing only the final pose while omitting the phrase boundaries

C. Increasing the total number of actions practiced at once

D. Dividing the phrase at random without preserving its movement order

**3. Which is a useful corrective loop?**

A. Retrieve the phrase repeatedly without checking it against the count sheet

B. Compare the phrase with the score once, then rehearse without correcting errors

C. Retrieve, compare with score, correct, retrieve again

D. Copy the phrase from the score continuously without attempting unaided recall

**4. Why test after a short break?**

A. To see whether the phrase can be retrieved beyond immediate repetition

B. To establish that one successful immediate repetition guarantees long-term retention

C. To confirm professional movement quality from recall alone

D. To demonstrate that the written score is no longer useful for checking errors

### Answer key with reasoning

**1. B.** Retrieval practice means attempting the phrase from memory without reading the score during the attempt. A and C keep the score visible, while D repeats observation without independent recall.

**2. A.** Chunking groups a sequence into meaningful manageable units while preserving order. B omits the sequence, C increases simultaneous load, and D divides randomly without respecting movement structure.

**3. C.** Retrieve, compare with the score, correct errors, and retrieve again creates a feedback loop. A omits comparison, B stops before correction, and D copies continuously without unaided recall.

**4. A.** A recall attempt after a short break tests whether the phrase can be retrieved beyond immediate repetition. B overclaims long-term retention, C confuses recall with professional technique, and D discards the score's ongoing error-checking value.

**Self-correction:** Explain any missed answer in your own words and repeat the associated movement chunk at a slower count. These questions check conceptual understanding, not externally validated technique.

## Repertory assignment

Submit a written count sheet and, if you choose, a private practice recording. Document starting and ending facing, supporting foot or seated support, the movement on each count, and one deliberate revision. For Lesson 03, submit a two-phrase (16-count) A–B retrieval log with one immediate and one delayed attempt, the counts corrected after feedback, and the result of the second unaided recall.

**Worked example:** “Without the score, I recalled A counts 1–8 but omitted B's turn on 13–14. I checked the written score, marked counts 11–16 twice, rested two minutes, and then recalled both phrases unaided. My log distinguishes an observed correction from a claim about long-term retention.”

**Rubric:** Accurate A–B 16-count unaided recall across immediate and post-break attempts 30%; controlled support and A→B transition 25%; complete count-by-count retrieval log with error locations 25%; specific score-based correction and documented second unaided attempt 20%. Self-scoring does not certify readiness for advanced physical skills.
