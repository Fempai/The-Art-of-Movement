## Learning goal and explanation

Distinguish rolling, fluttering, and undulating as movement descriptions; explore only comfortable preparatory versions without forcing abdominal contractions. Describe the weight support, pathway, rhythm, and difference between a beginner drill and a historically attributed movement.

**Concept distinction:** For this lesson, a **roll** describes an ordered progression through body regions; an **undulation** describes a traveling wave-like sequence, which may involve more than one torso region; and a **flutter** describes rapid repeated abdominal actions rather than a slow chest-to-pelvis pathway. These are **descriptive contrasts, not universal definitions across dance traditions**, and the terms may overlap in actual instruction. The guided counts 3–6 teach **only a slow forward-and-reverse sequence**. They do **not** practice rapid flutters, establish a complete belly roll or undulation, or authenticate any named tradition. A hand-traced version teaches pathway order, not abdominal isolation.

## Follow-along practice

**Warm up (4–5 minutes):** Walk or march gently, shift weight slowly, and mobilize ankles and shoulders in a comfortable range. Clear a nonslip space and keep a stable support nearby. Stop for pain, dizziness, numbness, or repeated loss of control.

**Guided count-by-count study (8–12 minutes):** Counts 1–2: settle in a comfortable supported stance or seat and breathe naturally. First **hand-trace** the sequence: count 3 mark the upper torso/chest position, count 4 mark the lower torso/pelvis position, count 5 retrace the lower-to-upper return beginning at the pelvis, and count 6 finish at the chest. Counts 7–8: relax and continue comfortable breathing. If this ordering is clear and movement feels comfortable, optionally explore a very small sequential torso intention on counts 3–6 without forcing spinal motion. The hand-traced pathway teaches order; it does not reproduce abdominal isolation or verify a complete belly roll, flutter, or undulation. Do not hold the breath or attempt forceful flutters.

**Three repetitions (5–8 minutes):** First mark slowly while saying each count; next identify the body part or contact point supporting each movement; finally vary one accent without increasing range. Rest between repetitions. If coordination breaks down, practice the components separately before recombining.

**Modification:** Sit in a stable nonrolling chair with both feet supported. On count 3 trace toward the chest with one hand; on 4 trace toward the pelvis; on 5 begin the return from pelvis toward chest; on 6 finish the return at chest. Keep the other arm supported and breathe naturally on counts 1–2 and 7–8. Omit abdominal action entirely if it is uncomfortable.

**Self-correction:** If your knees twist, return to parallel and reduce range. If the back compresses or balance becomes uncertain, shorten the pathway or remain seated. If you lose timing, clap eight counts and add only one movement at a time. Do not force visible isolations, rapid abdominal work, or extreme spinal motion.

**Progression check:** In each of two comfortable attempts, mark whether count 3 identifies chest, 4 pelvis, 5 pelvis beginning the reverse return, and 6 chest finishing the return. For each count record **correct order / incorrect order / unobservable** and whether the pathway was hand-traced or a small optional torso intention. Do not grade hand tracing as demonstrated abdominal isolation; stop or omit torso action if uncomfortable. Explain one observed difference and one limitation. This is a self-study checkpoint, not a claim of injury prevention or expert skill.

## Four-question knowledge check

**1. What distinguishes the lesson's small torso wave from a repeated pulse?**

A. It alternates between two fixed positions without a sequential return

B. It repeats the same chest accent on each count rather than tracing a return

C. It traces a chest-to-pelvis sequence and reverse return

D. It changes the timing of a single pulse without changing the order of body segments

**2. A learner feels back compression during the wave. Which response fits the lesson?**

A. Reduce the range slightly but continue the same compressive motion

B. Reduce or omit the torso action and trace the order with hands

C. Repeat only the forward sequence at the same uncomfortable range

D. Replace the wave with larger standing hip shifts despite continued back discomfort

**3. Which statement about the Week 13 drill is best supported?**

A. It introduces a preparatory sequence, not a complete belly roll or flutter

B. It demonstrates a complete belly roll because the torso segments moved in sequence

C. It shows that the source video uses this exact sequence even though playback is unverified

D. It establishes one consistent tempo for all torso-wave variations

**4. What is the best way to check whether the return path is distinct?**

A. Repeat counts 1–2 without examining the forward-to-reverse transition

B. Mark counts 3–6 but describe only the forward direction

C. Repeat the full phrase while observing only the end position

D. Mark counts 3–6 slowly and describe the forward and reverse order

### Answer key and explanations

**1. C.** The small wave follows a chest-to-pelvis sequence and reverse return. A and B describe repeated fixed-position accents, while D changes timing without preserving the ordered segment pathway.

**2. B.** Reduce or omit the compressive torso action and trace the sequence with hands, seated if helpful. A and C retain the uncomfortable motion, while D substitutes larger standing movement despite discomfort.

**3. A.** The lesson introduces a preparatory low-range sequence, not a complete belly roll or flutter. B overstates technique, C assumes unverified video matching, and D claims one tempo suits all variants.

**4. D.** Slowly marking counts 3–6 while describing forward and reverse order tests the return pathway. A omits the transition, B describes only the forward sequence, and C checks only the endpoint.

**Assessment limit:** These original questions assess introductory understanding, not professional dance competence. Review the explanation and retry after any missed answer.
