## Learning goal and explanation

Distinguish rolling, fluttering, and undulating as movement descriptions; explore only comfortable preparatory versions without forcing abdominal contractions. Describe the weight support, pathway, rhythm, and difference between a beginner drill and a historically attributed movement.

**Concept distinction:** This low-risk wave preparation is not a demonstration of full belly rolls, rapid flutters, or any named tradition.

## Follow-along practice

**Warm up (4–5 minutes):** Walk or march gently, shift weight slowly, and mobilize ankles and shoulders in a comfortable range. Clear a nonslip space and keep a stable support nearby. Stop for pain, dizziness, numbness, or repeated loss of control.

**Guided count-by-count study (8–12 minutes):** Counts 1–2 settle in comfortable posture; 3–4 make a tiny chest-to-pelvis sequential torso shift within an easy range; 5–6 return in reverse order; 7–8 rest and breathe normally. Observe how a sequence differs from a repeated pulse; do not attempt forceful flutters.

**Three repetitions (5–8 minutes):** First mark slowly while saying each count; next identify the body part or contact point supporting each movement; finally vary one accent without increasing range. Rest between repetitions. If coordination breaks down, practice the components separately before recombining.

**Modification:** Trace the sequence with the hands while seated, omitting abdominal action entirely.

**Self-correction:** If your knees twist, return to parallel and reduce range. If the back compresses or balance becomes uncertain, shorten the pathway or remain seated. If you lose timing, clap eight counts and add only one movement at a time. Do not force visible isolations, rapid abdominal work, or extreme spinal motion.

**Progression check:** Repeat the adapted phrase twice at a steady count, identify one observable change, and explain one limitation. This is a self-study checkpoint, not a claim of injury prevention or expert skill.

## Four-question knowledge check

**1. What distinguishes the lesson's small torso wave from a repeated pulse?**

A. It must be performed quickly

B. It requires a deep backbend

C. It traces a chest-to-pelvis sequence and reverse return

D. It proves the dancer can perform a flutter

**2. A learner feels back compression during the wave. Which response fits the lesson?**

A. Increase the range to complete the shape

B. Reduce or omit the torso action and trace the order with hands

C. Hold the breath until the movement finishes

D. Attempt faster abdominal contractions

**3. Which statement about the Week 13 drill is best supported?**

A. It introduces a preparatory sequence, not a complete belly roll or flutter

B. It verifies the history of a named dance

C. It establishes that rapid flutters are safe

D. It proves all undulations share one technique

**4. What is the best way to check whether the return path is distinct?**

A. Only assess how large the wave looks

B. Remove the rest counts

C. Move directly to maximum speed

D. Mark counts 3–6 slowly and describe the forward and reverse order

### Answer key and explanations

**1. C.** The counts describe a small sequential shift and reverse return; a pulse repeats an accent rather than tracing the same ordered pathway.

**2. B.** The hand-traced seated alternative retains the sequencing concept while avoiding the uncomfortable torso action.

**3. A.** This original low-range sequence is preparatory; neither advanced abdominal technique nor cultural attribution follows from completing it.

**4. D.** Slow marking makes the chest-to-pelvis order and its reverse observable; greater speed or range would obscure the lesson's intended comparison.

**Assessment limit:** These original questions assess introductory understanding, not professional dance competence. Review the explanation and retry after any missed answer.
