# HIP101 · Lesson 01 — Groove: Pulse, Weight, and Personal Timing

**Learner-facing draft | 40–55 minutes.** The website is the instructor for this introductory lesson. This is not a certification of technique or an endorsement of unsupported cultural attributions. Scholarly metadata and deep links still require independent verification.

## Learn the idea

Groove is not a single step that every hip-hop dancer performs identically. In this introductory lab, groove means a recurring relationship among musical pulse, weight shift, body movement, and personal rhythmic choice. You can reproduce the same step pattern while changing the weight, accent, or timing and thereby change its character. Hip-hop dance practices have developed through communities, parties, crews, and exchange; they should not be reduced to a generic exercise divorced from people and history. Joseph Schloss's ethnographic account of breaking emphasizes practitioners and community knowledge, while Jeff Chang's cultural history situates hip-hop in broader social and political contexts. Neither source proves that this original exercise is a universal hip-hop foundation.

**Vocabulary in practice:** A *count* is a numbered timing reference, a *pulse* is a recurring temporal reference, an *accent* is a point of emphasis, and a *weight shift* is a change in how your body is supported. These are working descriptions for the exercises below, not universal definitions of a dance tradition.

## Follow-along practice

**Prepare (4 minutes):** Clear a nonslip space. March gently, shift weight between feet, and move shoulders in a comfortable range. Keep knees soft; avoid forced twists, maximal knee bends, and unstable furniture. Stop for pain, dizziness, numbness, or repeated loss of control.

**Practice (10–15 minutes):** Stand with feet comfortably apart and knees soft. On count 1 shift weight right with a small downward pulse; 2 return toward center; 3 shift left with a small pulse; 4 return; 5 step right; 6 bring left foot toward right without forcing it to close; 7 step left; 8 settle. Repeat, accenting counts 2 and 4 with a gentle shoulder movement. Keep the pulse shallow. For a seated version, shift pressure gently between sitting bones and use torso and arms to mark the pulse.

**What to notice:** Version A places emphasis on each step. Version B keeps the same foot pathway but emphasizes the moments between steps with a shoulder accent. Ask what changed in timing and weight rather than declaring one more authentic.

**Apply to Undercurrent (5–8 minutes):** Begin with the original step–gather–reach–recover phrase. Keep the eight-count framework, but replace one accent or weight transfer with today's concept. Perform your new version twice. Write the counts and explain what you intentionally changed. An original exercise is not a historical claim.

## Fix common difficulties

| Observation | Possible explanation | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| You bounce so deeply that your knees hurt | The range is excessive for this task | Use a smaller pulse or seated marking |
| You rush ahead of the count | You may be anticipating rather than listening to the pulse | Count aloud and reduce footwork |
| Your groove looks like a rigid series of poses | You may be holding each position instead of allowing transitions | Try a continuous, comfortable shift of weight |

**Progression checkpoint:** You can maintain an eight-count pulse, reproduce the phrase twice in a comfortable range, explain the difference between observation and interpretation, and avoid presenting a personal invention as an attributed historical step. A written score cannot verify expert performance quality.

## Knowledge check

**1. What is the best description of groove in this lesson?**

A. A single footwork pattern performed at the same tempo in every song

B. A relationship among pulse, weight, and movement

C. The arm pathway alone, without attention to pulse or weight

D. A sequence of poses that ignores the beat and weight changes

**2. What changes when the same steps are accented differently?**

A. The movement's anatomical joint structure, even if the steps remain the same

B. The original community context of the dance, even though only the accents change

C. The rhythmic character of the phrase

D. The exact foot placements, even though the same steps are repeated

**3. Why is community context relevant?**

A. Dance practices emerge through people and social settings

B. Community context determines one universal groove for every participant

C. Learning the counts alone fully establishes the cultural origin of a phrase

D. A dancer's isolated movement recording is sufficient to reconstruct every social influence

**4. Which is a useful beginner modification?**

A. A larger standing bounce with the same difficulty maintaining control

B. A seated arm gesture that omits the planned rhythmic pulse

C. A standing foot pattern that retains the steps but removes the pulse and weight awareness

D. A shallow pulse or seated weight shift

### Answers and explanations

**1. B.** Groove here is an embodied rhythmic relationship, not a single codified move.

**2. C.** Accent and timing change phrasing even if the step sequence stays the same.

**3. A.** Understanding people and settings avoids treating the practice as an ahistorical exercise.

**4. D.** The modification retains rhythm and weight awareness while reducing load.

**Important assessment limitation:** The questions test introductory conceptual understanding and have not been psychometrically validated. Answer positions vary across these four questions; a future interactive question bank should also randomize options and explain incorrect choices. Retake any missed item after reviewing its explanation.

## Independent assignment

Produce a two-phrase, sixteen-count original groove study. Provide (1) a count-by-count movement score, (2) 250–350 words explaining your rhythmic choices, (3) one precise cultural or historical claim supported by an appropriate source, and (4) one claim you deliberately avoid making because the evidence is insufficient.

**Worked example:** “I shifted right on count one and added a shoulder accent on the 'and' after two. That rhythmic placement is observable in my count sheet. I cannot conclude that the gesture originated with a particular crew without documentary or practitioner evidence.”

**Rubric:** Rhythmic description 25%; clear and repeatable movement design 25%; cultural precision and attribution 25%; evidence, limitations, and reflection 25%. Do not grade a learner's cultural authenticity from their body or appearance.
