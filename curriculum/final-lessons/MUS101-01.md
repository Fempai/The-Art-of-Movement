# MUS101 · Lesson 01 — Pulse, Meter, and Counting in 4/4

**Self-study course | 35–50 minutes | learner-facing revision, final source/link/browser acceptance pending.** No musical instrument, teacher, or ballet barre is required. You may clap or tap while seated.

## Learning objectives

Define the key concept accurately, demonstrate it using a steady count, analyze an original eight-count phrase, distinguish direct timing observations from interpretations, and explain your answers to four knowledge questions.

## Lesson: read and reason

A **pulse** is a recurring reference point that listeners can use to coordinate movement. **Meter** is the grouping of beats into recurring patterns, often with differences in emphasis. In **4/4**, a written measure contains the duration of four quarter notes. The upper 4 names four beat units per measure; the lower 4 designates the quarter note as the notated beat unit. That does not mean every piece in 4/4 has a loud sound on each beat, nor that every dance style uses identical accents. Dancers often count eight beats across two measures of 4/4. An eight-count dance phrase is a teaching convention, not a universal musical unit. For example, the rhythm of a melody can cross the bar line even while the meter remains unchanged.

**Worked calculation and example:** At 60 beats per minute, one beat lasts 1 second. Four beats take 4 seconds, and an eight-count takes 8 seconds. At 120 beats per minute, an eight-count lasts 4 seconds. These calculations assume each dance count corresponds to one quarter-note beat.

**Common misunderstanding:** If you mistake every drum hit for a new beat, you may count twice as fast when an instrument plays extra notes. First find the recurring pulse, then listen for the subdivisions and accents around it.

## Follow-along rhythm laboratory

**Preparation:** Find a quiet space, sit or stand comfortably, and tap a steady beat for 30 seconds. You can use your own spoken count; no copyrighted recording is required. Do not force breathing or physical range. If stepping causes discomfort, remain seated and tap hands or feet gently.

**Guided practice (10–15 minutes):** Choose an unhurried pulse and say 'ONE two three four | ONE two three four'. Tap one hand four times, slightly emphasizing the first tap. Then count '1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8' over two groups of four while stepping or marking seated. For eight counts: 1 step right, 2 gather, 3 step left, 4 pause, 5 reach right, 6 continue reach, 7 recover, 8 pause. Repeat four cycles. Now emphasize count 3 rather than count 1 with a clap. Notice that your accent changed, but the count structure did not.

**Troubleshooting:** If you lose the pulse, stop adding accents and tap only the main beat. If the subdivisions become confusing, count aloud with one hand marking beats and the other marking 'ands'. If a movement transition is unstable, keep both feet on the ground and mark the phrase with your hands. Correct timing is not a reason to ignore pain or dizziness.

**Transfer to Undercurrent:** Keep the step–gather–reach–recover motif, then change exactly one rhythmic feature: where the accent lands, how many beats the reach occupies, or where an offbeat gesture appears. Write the original and revised counts side by side. Do not assume that a musical count dictates an emotional meaning.

## Knowledge check — answer before looking below

**1. What does the lower 4 in 4/4 specify?**

A. Four instruments

B. Quarter note as the notated beat unit

C. Four measures

D. Four dance styles

**2. An eight-count phrase at 120 quarter-note beats per minute lasts how long?**

A. 8 seconds

B. 2 seconds

C. 4 seconds

D. 16 seconds

**3. What is a meter?**

A. A recurring grouping of beats

B. The loudest instrument

C. The length of a dancer's stride

D. An individual drum stroke

**4. If you change which count you clap on, what necessarily changes?**

A. The written time signature

B. The tempo

C. The location of your accent

D. The number of measures

### Correct answers with reasoning

**1. B.** The denominator identifies the note value assigned one beat in the written meter.

**2. C.** Each beat lasts 60/120 = 0.5 seconds, so eight beats take 4 seconds.

**3. A.** Meter organizes beats into repeating patterns; the individual sounds may not coincide with every beat.

**4. C.** The accent changes; the meter and tempo can remain the same.

**Self-assessment:** Four correct demonstrates comprehension of the concepts sampled here. If you miss an item, work through the arithmetic or clapping example again and retry. These questions are instructor-authored and not psychometrically validated.

## Independent assignment

**Deliverable:** Create a 32-count movement-and-rhythm score. Mark beats, any subdivisions, weight shifts or seated equivalents, and at least two intentional accents. Include a 250–350-word explanation of one rhythmic revision and one claim you can verify from counts versus one artistic interpretation.

**Example:** “At 120 BPM, my eight-count phrase takes four seconds. I moved the hand accent from beat three to the 'and' after beat two without changing the beat rate. The new emphasis is observable in the score; whether it sounds surprising depends on the musical context.”
