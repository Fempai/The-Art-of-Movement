# PER201 · Lesson 01 — Audience Focus: Directing Attention With Gaze and Orientation

**Performance & Storytelling | 40–55 minutes | independent learner.** This is an authored lesson revision, not a professionally adjudicated performance assessment. Bibliographic and live-browser audits remain outstanding.

## Learn: the performance principle

By the end, identify a visible expressive variable, perform two contrasting versions of a phrase, and critique a recording without confusing observable facts with presumed audience emotions.

In performance, focus is an intentional organization of attention. A dancer can look toward a hand, across the room, toward an imagined person, or toward an audience area. Gaze, head orientation, torso facing, and travel direction can be coordinated or contrasted. Looking toward an audience is not inherently more expressive than looking away. What matters is whether the choice is consistent with the work's intention and legible from a given viewing position. A solo recording can show where your head points but cannot reveal where every viewer actually looked.

## Guided performance studio

**Prepare (5 minutes):** Clear a nonslip area and choose a safe front-facing reference point. Warm up with easy steps, gentle arm reaches, and comfortable head movements. Seated or small-range marking is acceptable; no leaps or forced movements are required.

**Perform (15 minutes):** Mark a front-facing audience position with a safe stationary object across the room. Perform the eight-count Undercurrent motif: 1 step right, 2 settle, 3–4 gather, 5–6 diagonal reach, 7 recover, 8 stillness. Version A: keep gaze on your reaching hand for counts 5–6. Version B: look toward the imagined audience on count 5, keeping the arm pathway identical. Version C: begin looking down, then lift gaze on count 7. **Seated alternative:** On a stable nonrolling chair with feet supported, use 1 small rightward sitting-pressure shift, 2 settle, 3–4 gather, 5–6 diagonal right-arm reach, 7 recover, 8 stillness. Apply the same A/B/C gaze destinations and counts without requiring a standing step. For C, keep the gaze comfortably lowered through 6, then lift it on 7; do not force the neck downward. Perform each twice with comfortable neck movement. Compare video or written notes for the exact count when the gaze changes.

**Observe and revise (5 minutes):** If gaze changes make the phrase unsteady, keep your head forward and shift attention using only eye direction. If the three versions are indistinguishable, hold the gaze destination through a full count. Do not equate averted gaze with fear or direct gaze with confidence without evidence.

**Undercurrent application:** Record the count, facing, gaze, pathway, and expressive intention separately. Ask what actually changed between versions. If using video, preserve the same camera position and note what falls outside the frame.

## Knowledge check

**1. Which is a focus choice?**

A. The direction the dancer travels without specifying where the eyes look

B. Looking toward the reaching hand on count 5

C. The beat on which the supporting foot changes without a gaze choice

D. The final arm position without a chosen visual target

**2. What can a recording most directly establish?**

A. The reason the performer chose to look toward the hand

B. Whether the dancer intended the gaze to convey confidence

C. Visible head and gaze orientation

D. How every future viewer will interpret the head turn

**3. What is a safer alternative when turning the head causes imbalance?**

A. Keep the head forward and adjust eye direction

B. Keep the same unstable head turn but shorten the arm reach

C. Increase the turn speed while keeping the same narrow base

D. Maintain the full head rotation and remove nearby support

**4. Which statement is defensible?**

A. A direct gaze always communicates confidence regardless of context

B. Looking away necessarily communicates weakness to every viewer

C. Looking toward a hand guarantees every viewer will follow it

D. Different focus choices may change visual emphasis

### Answers and rationales

**1. B.** Looking toward the reaching hand on count 5 specifies both a visual target and timing, so it is a focus choice. Travel direction (A), support-foot timing (C), and an arm endpoint without gaze (D) describe other choreographic variables.

**2. C.** Video can document visible head and approximate gaze orientation when the face is in frame. It cannot establish the performer's private reason (A), prove the intended emotional message (B), or determine every future viewer's interpretation (D).

**3. A.** Keeping the head stable while changing eye direction reduces the balance demand of head rotation and retains a focus task. Preserving an unstable turn (B), accelerating it (C), or removing support (D) fails to address the balance concern.

**4. D.** Changing focus can alter where visual emphasis falls, but the audience's interpretation depends on context. Direct gaze does not always signal confidence (A), looking away does not always imply weakness (B), and following the hand is not guaranteed (C).

**Self-review:** For any missed question, label the alternatives as observation, interpretation, or unsupported generalization. This knowledge check is original and not a validated audience-response measure.

## Independent assignment

Perform the taught eight-count motif in three gaze versions: A gaze follows the reaching hand on counts 5–6; B gaze moves toward the imagined audience on count 5 while the arm pathway remains unchanged; C begins with gaze down and lifts it on count 7. Score all eight counts for each, marking head or eye orientation, facing, support, and exact gaze-change count. Compare at least three observable differences across the three versions, name your intended focus effect without claiming an audience response, and revise one cue. Submit a 250–350-word critique using footage or a written observation grid.

**Worked example:** “In A, my gaze followed my reaching hand through counts 5–6. In B, I looked toward the audience marker on count 5 while my arm followed the same diagonal path. In C, my gaze began downward and lifted on 7. The recorded gaze destinations and timing differed; I cannot conclude where an actual audience would look.”

**Rubric:** Observable specificity 30%; reproducible performance choices 25%; clear distinction between intention and interpretation 25%; justified revision and reflection 20%. A self-score does not establish professional stage readiness.
