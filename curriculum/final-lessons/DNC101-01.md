## What you will learn

Define the featured movement; identify the supporting leg, working leg, and relevant joint actions; reproduce a small-range phrase with counts; identify and correct common beginner errors; explain how musical timing changes the effect.

## Lesson: read before practicing

Tendu means 'stretched' in French ballet terminology. In a basic battement tendu, the working foot glides along the floor until the leg and foot lengthen, then returns along the same path. The supporting leg bears weight; the working foot does not become a second weight-bearing leg at full extension. This is a coordination exercise, not a test of maximal turnout. In a parallel adaptation, the same principle—controlled sliding, lengthening, and return—can be studied without forcing rotation. Jazz classes also use tendu-based foot articulation, but style and rhythm may differ. Ballet terminology developed through particular European court and theatrical traditions and should not be presented as the origin of all dance technique.

**Why this matters:** In a self-taught setting, a name alone is not instruction. You need to know which foot supports your weight, what pathway the working limb takes, and what would count as a repeatable action. A shape that resembles a photograph is not proof that you used the intended movement. A slower tempo often helps you observe transitions, but it is not automatically the correct performance tempo for every style.

**Preparation:** Stand with feet parallel or in a comfortable small natural turnout. Keep knees comfortably unlocked and a light hand on a wall or securely fixed support if needed. Wear socks only if the floor provides safe traction; otherwise use suitable footwear. Begin with 3–5 minutes of comfortable marching, ankle circles, and small knee bends. Do not force range, and stop if you experience pain, dizziness, numbness, or repeated loss of control.

## Follow-along movement instruction

- **Count 1: Shift your weight clearly onto the left foot without locking the knee.**
- **Count 2: Slide the right foot forward along the floor, keeping contact through the toes as the foot lengthens.**
- **Count 3: Hold the extended line briefly; the right foot remains lightly touching rather than bearing your weight.**
- **Count 4: Reverse the pathway, returning the right foot to the starting point.**
- **Count 5–8: Repeat the same sequence to the side, staying within a small comfortable range.**

**Repeat the phrase:** Perform it three times at a comfortable pace. On the first repetition, say the counts. On the second, attend to foot contact and support. On the third, make the phrasing intentional without increasing the range.

**Correct-versus-misleading comparison:** A controlled tendu uses an intentional outward and return pathway. If you throw the foot away and snap it back, you may reproduce the endpoint without practicing the articulation or weight distribution that defines this exercise.

**Transfer to Undercurrent:** Use the original eight-count step–gather–reach–recover motif. Replace the gather or transition with a small version of today's movement, then explain whether the change affects timing, support, or the audience's visual focus. You are composing an original study, not copying choreography from a source.

## Troubleshooting when practicing alone

| What you notice | What might be happening | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| The working hip hikes upward | You may be trying to reach farther than the current range allows | Shorten the tendu and keep the pelvis comfortably organized |
| You fall toward the sliding foot | Weight may be leaving the supporting leg too early | Use fixed support and make the reach smaller |
| Your toes curl tightly | You may be gripping instead of lengthening the foot | Relax, reduce pressure, and trace the floor gently |

**Success check:** You can identify your support foot, demonstrate the movement twice at the same counts in a comfortable range, and explain one difference between the intended pathway and a common error. This does not certify injury safety or advanced technique.

## Knowledge check — try before reading the answers

**1. What is the defining feature of a basic tendu?**

A. A high jump

B. A sliding extension and return along the floor

C. A forced split

D. A full turn

**2. Which leg primarily supports the dancer during a right-foot tendu?**

A. The right working leg

B. Neither leg

C. The left standing leg

D. Both legs equally at full extension

**3. Which adaptation retains the core learning goal without forcing turnout?**

A. A small parallel tendu

B. A maximal external rotation

C. A jump with twisted feet

D. An unsupported high extension

**4. Why should the working foot return along its pathway?**

A. To develop repeatable articulation and spatial accuracy

B. Because all dances forbid curved paths

C. To guarantee injury prevention

D. To increase turnout instantly

### Answers and explanations

**1. B.** The working foot traces a deliberate path along the floor and returns.

**2. C.** The standing leg supports the weight while the working foot extends lightly.

**3. A.** A parallel tendu preserves controlled foot articulation and pathway.

**4. A.** The deliberate return develops coordination; it does not guarantee any medical outcome.

**Study rule:** If you miss an item, read the explanation, mark the relevant movement at a small range, and retry without looking. These knowledge questions are instructor-authored and not psychometrically validated. A quiz score cannot establish movement safety.

## Assignment and worked example

**Task:** Record two forward and two side tendus, then annotate how your weight and working-foot contact change. Compare the pathway in ballet counts with a jazz-style accent without claiming the styles are interchangeable. Write 250–350 words explaining your movement choices, what you observed, what you could not verify, and one source-supported principle relevant to the exercise.

**Worked example:** “I kept my weight on the left foot during the right-foot tendu. On count four I returned the foot along the same line. When I moved the accent to count three, the endpoint stayed similar but the phrasing changed. I cannot infer muscle activation from appearance alone.”

**Rubric:** Terminology and accuracy 25%; counts and movement description 25%; analysis and revision 25%; appropriate source use and limitations 25%. The rubric evaluates academic reasoning separately from technical skill.
