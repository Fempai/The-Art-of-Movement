# RES501 · Lesson 01 — From Artistic Curiosity to a Researchable Question

**Graduate-level dance research methods | 50–70 minutes | independent study.** This learner-facing revision has not completed scholarly passage verification, expert review, or browser acceptance testing.

## Lecture: research reasoning

**Outcomes:** Define the method in this lesson, use it in a small inquiry, separate evidence from interpretation, and document the limits of any conclusion.

A research question identifies what you want to know, about whom or what, under which conditions, and by what kind of evidence. 'Does dance make people emotional?' is too broad and assumes a measurable uniform response. A narrower self-study question is: 'In my own 32-count Undercurrent phrase, how consistently can I identify the count on which a diagonal reach begins across five rehearsals?' This question names an observable event, a defined sample of performances, and a possible counting method. It does not establish why a movement occurs or how an audience interprets it. Exploratory qualitative questions may instead ask how a performer describes the experience of varying a phrase; the evidence then consists of situated accounts rather than universal measurements.

## Guided research laboratory

**Preparation (5 minutes):** Create a dated research notebook. State the research question before gathering observations. If movement is involved, warm up gently, use a safe space, and choose comfortable small-range movements.

**Apply the method (20–25 minutes):** Write three questions about Undercurrent: descriptive ('On which count does the reach begin?'), comparative ('Does the count differ between slow and moderate tempos?'), and interpretive ('How do I describe my experience of the delayed reach?'). For each, specify a unit of analysis, evidence source, observation method, and one limitation. Choose the descriptive question for a pilot. Mark five performances, recording the first visible reach count and whether the score was consulted. Do not alter the research question after seeing results without recording the change.

**Troubleshoot your reasoning:** If a question asks whether a movement is 'better,' define what better means before collecting evidence. If you have only your own performances, describe the sample as one performer rather than generalizing to dancers broadly.

**Connect to Undercurrent:** Treat the original step–gather–reach–recover phrase as a bounded study object. Describe its count score, the exact feature under investigation, and whether your evidence comes from rehearsal, video, testimony, or published research. Do not call a personal impression a universal result.

## Knowledge check

**1. Which question is most directly researchable with a solo count log?**

A. Do five reach-onset trials prove that the movement causes joy in all dancers?

B. On which count does my reach begin across five trials?

C. Does my own count log establish how unseen audiences interpret the reach?

D. Do five trials establish which dance style produces the best emotional response?

**2. What is a unit of analysis?**

A. The specific event, person, or performance being examined

B. The topic of the study without specifying what each recorded observation represents

C. The conclusion the researcher hopes to reach before collecting observations

D. The total number of trials without specifying the event measured in each

**3. What is a limitation of one dancer's five trials?**

A. Repeating one person's trials establishes how all dancers perform the movement

B. Five observations alone establish that the chosen count caused the reach timing

C. They cannot represent all dancers

D. Recording the same person repeatedly removes all observer and measurement bias

**4. What should happen if the research question changes after data collection begins?**

A. Rewrite the notebook so the revised question appears to have been planned from the outset

B. Discard earlier trials without reporting why the inclusion rule changed

C. Present the revised analysis as preregistered even though it was not

D. Document the revision and its timing

### Answer key and explanations

**1. B.** The reach onset can be defined as a visible event and logged by count across five trials. Five trials cannot prove a universal emotional effect (A), establish unseen audience interpretations (C), or rank styles by audience response (D).

**2. A.** A unit of analysis identifies the event, person, or performance each observation concerns. A broad topic (B), desired conclusion (C), or trial total without an observed event (D) does not specify the unit being examined.

**3. C.** Repeated observations from one performer describe that performer's trials under the recorded conditions; they do not represent all dancers. Repetition does not establish population-wide patterns (A), isolate a cause (B), or eliminate bias (D).

**4. D.** Recording when and why the question changed distinguishes planned analysis from later exploration. Rewriting the notebook (A), silently dropping earlier trials (B), or falsely calling the revised plan preregistered (C) obscures the actual research process.

**Feedback cycle:** Explain why each missed alternative overreaches or misidentifies evidence. Then revise one claim in your notebook. These original questions check concepts, not graduate credit or standardized research competence.

## Independent assignment

Submit a research artifact specific to this lesson (question matrix, evidence matrix, observation log, or proposal) and a 250–350-word methodological reflection. State at least two limitations and one change you would make before repeating the inquiry. **Lesson 04 capstone:** a 600–800-word **research proposal** with a question, sources, procedure, operational definitions, analysis plan, reflexivity, ethics, and limits.

**Worked example:** “I defined reach onset as the first visible outward hand motion after the gather. In trial 2, the hand was outside the frame, so onset was coded 'not observable.' I will widen the camera framing before collecting more trials. This adjustment improves documentation; it does not establish a causal relationship.”

**Rubric:** Question and method alignment 25%; evidence quality and traceability 30%; transparent limitations and reflexivity 25%; organization and justified next steps 20%. Do not award credit for unsupported causal claims or fabricated source details.
