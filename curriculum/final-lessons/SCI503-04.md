# SCI503 · Lesson 04 — Fatigue, Retention, and a Safe Learning Study

**Motor Learning & Dance Science | graduate-level independent study | 45–60 minutes.** Learner-facing revision; source audit, expert review, and browser testing remain outstanding. Do not intentionally train to exhaustion or push through pain.

## Lecture: explain the learning mechanism

**Learning outcomes:** Distinguish observed performance from inferred learning, apply one defined practice method, collect repeatable movement observations, and identify what the data cannot prove.

Fatigue can change movement quality, attention, and the ability to execute a learned sequence, but the pattern differs among people and tasks. A fatigued performance is not necessarily evidence that learning was lost. A safe personal protocol should record sleep, perceived exertion, discomfort, and rest without deliberately pushing to exhaustion. A retention test after a rest period is more informative about recall than repeatedly performing under increasing fatigue. This lesson integrates variability, retrieval, and feedback into a small descriptive study with a preregistered plan and honest limits.

## Guided movement-science laboratory

**Prepare:** Warm up with gentle walking or seated weight shifts. Choose a safe, small-range version of the eight-count Undercurrent step–gather–reach–recover motif.

**Protocol (15–20 minutes):** Create a baseline session with a gentle five-minute warm-up. Perform three comfortable Undercurrent retrieval trials without looking at the score. **Define the four chunks before scoring: 1–2 step or supported seated shift and settle, 3–4 gather, 5–6 reach beginning on 5, 7–8 return and controlled finish. Score each chunk 1 only if its action and timing match; otherwise 0, with 'uncertain' recorded separately instead of guessed.** Record correct action chunks (0–4), onset count, balance corrections, and perceived effort on a 0–10 scale. Rest two minutes, then perform one more trial and note whether the score changed. Do not intentionally induce exhaustion. Schedule day-1, day-3, and day-7 recall checks using the same scoring criteria and comfortable warm-up. For each check, log actual date, time since last practice, and whether prompts were used. Make a simple line plot or table of accurate chunks by day; missing data remain missing.

**Troubleshooting and limitations:** If perceived effort rises sharply or technique becomes unsafe, stop and log the reason. If a delayed check is worse, consider competing explanations such as unfamiliarity, fatigue, or inconsistent conditions; do not declare that learning disappeared. If the schedule is interrupted, document the deviation.

**Research integrity check:** Record the planned trial count, actual trial count, condition, prompts, and deviations. Do not change scoring definitions after seeing outcomes without documenting the change. A solo log can help direct practice but cannot establish general causal effects.

## Knowledge check

**1. What is a defensible interpretation of one tired rehearsal?**

A. One tired attempt proves that previously learned timing has been permanently forgotten

B. Performance may be affected; learning cannot be inferred from one attempt

C. Slower timing after rehearsal establishes that the learner lacks motivation

D. One person's tired trial establishes the same fatigue response for all dancers

**2. Which protocol is appropriate for a beginner?**

A. Continue until the learner cannot reproduce the phrase, regardless of symptoms

B. Keep the planned workload even when discomfort or dizziness appears

C. Use comfortable trials with rest and symptom monitoring

D. Increase impact demands at the end of the session to make the fatigue effect visible

**3. What should be done with a missed day-7 check?**

A. Record it as missing or log the actual later date

B. Copy the planned day-7 date onto a score collected on day 9

C. Estimate day-7 recall from the earlier check without observing it

D. Remove the missed check from the timeline so the record appears complete

**4. What is the capstone's strongest supported claim?**

A. One learner's recall pattern establishes how all dancers retain movement

B. Changes in timing across sessions reveal a clinical motor diagnosis

C. A higher delayed score proves that spacing alone caused the improvement

D. It describes one learner's performance and recall under recorded conditions

### Answer key and explanation

**1. B.** A tired rehearsal may show temporarily altered execution, but one attempt cannot establish lasting loss of learning. Permanent forgetting (A), lack of motivation (C), and a universal response across dancers (D) all exceed the observed evidence.

**2. C.** Comfortable repetitions, adequate rest, and symptom monitoring permit observation without intentionally inducing unsafe fatigue. Continuing until failure (A), ignoring dizziness or discomfort (B), and increasing impact to force fatigue (D) introduce avoidable risk.

**3. A.** A missed day-7 check should be recorded as missing or completed later with its true date so elapsed time remains accurate. Backdating a day-9 score (B), inventing recall (C), or hiding the missed session (D) misrepresents the observation record.

**4. D.** The capstone can describe one learner's recorded timing and recall under specified conditions. It cannot generalize to all dancers (A), diagnose a clinical condition (B), or prove spacing alone caused improvement (C) without a different study design.

**Self-review:** Explain why any missed alternative confuses outcome with process, immediate performance with learning, or observation with causal inference. These are original teaching questions, not validated cognitive or clinical assessments.

## Independent assignment

Prepare a **prospective personal learning study** with a dated baseline (three unprompted eight-count retrieval trials, two-minute rest, one post-rest trial) and scheduled day-1, day-3, and day-7 retention checks. Predefine the 0–4 correct-chunk score, reach onset, balance corrections, effort 0–10, prompts, and missing-data notation. Submit the baseline observations and an empty, dated follow-up table or graph template now; **a completed retention report is due only after the later sessions actually happen**. Once observed, add the real data, deviations, a 250–350-word interpretation, one competing explanation, and a safe next practice plan. Do not deliberately fatigue yourself or manufacture follow-up outcomes.

**Worked example:** “My hypothetical baseline scores are 2, 3, and 3 correct chunks out of 4, with a post-rest score of 3. I record perceived effort and whether I consulted the score. The day-1, day-3, and day-7 cells are blank until those dates. A later drop would prompt consideration of sleep, prompting, and context—not a claim that learning was erased.”

**Rubric:** Prospective protocol and defined measures 30%; accurate baseline, follow-up and missing-data records 30%; performance/retention distinction and competing explanations 25%; safety and justified future plan 15%. No score rewards exhaustion or extreme range.
