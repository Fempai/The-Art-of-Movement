# MOV101 · Lesson 01 — Joints, Movement, and Alignment

**Status: Independently teachable lesson; browser and learner acceptance testing pending.** Estimated self-study time: 35–50 minutes. No ballet barre required. This is foundational dance education, not an assessment of injury or joint health.

## What you will be able to do

By the end, you will (1) distinguish a visible dance shape from movement at a particular joint, (2) identify hip flexion, extension, abduction, and rotation in simple actions, (3) perform and annotate an original eight-count phrase, and (4) explain why dancers with different anatomy may not achieve identical turnout.

### 1. Read this first: what does a joint do?

A joint is the place where bones meet. Many dance actions involve synovial joints, where bones articulate with support from cartilage, ligaments, a joint capsule, and surrounding muscles. The hip is a ball-and-socket joint: the rounded head of the femur articulates with the socket of the pelvis. That design permits motion in several directions, but actual available motion varies with bone shape, soft tissue, and the task. The International Association for Dance Medicine & Science (IADMS) explains how individual hip anatomy contributes to differences in turnout [1].

**The essential distinction:** the position of your leg in a mirror is not the same measurement as the movement of your femur relative to your pelvis. Imagine raising your right knee. If your pelvis tilts backward or your trunk leans, the visible height of your knee can change even when hip motion does not change by the same amount. Researchers use methods such as motion analysis, force measurement, and muscle-activity measurement to study these interactions; a photograph alone cannot isolate them [2, 3].

A *reference segment* is the body part against which another part's motion is described. To describe hip flexion, compare the thigh with the pelvis. To describe the movement of the whole body through the room, compare the body with the floor and surroundings. You can be moving in the room while maintaining nearly the same hip angle, or remain in one place while the hip angle changes.

### 2. Learn the four basic hip actions

- **Flexion:** thigh moves forward relative to pelvis, as in a small knee lift.
- **Extension:** thigh moves backward relative to pelvis, as in a small step behind.
- **Abduction:** thigh moves away from the body's midline, as in a small side leg lift.
- **External rotation:** thigh rotates outward at the hip, contributing to turnout.

These are simplified descriptions. Real choreography combines actions and may involve pelvis, trunk, knee, ankle, and foot adjustments. In particular, **turnout is not simply how far apart your toes point**. The 2025 IADMS resource paper distinguishes anatomical hip rotation from the total visible position and describes individual variation [1].

**Worked example:** Two dancers each point their feet outward. One rotates comfortably at the hip. The other twists the feet outward while the knees remain oriented differently. Their foot positions might look similar, but the underlying mechanics differ. The correct response is not to demand a particular angle. For this introductory exercise, choose a comfortable position and do not twist the feet against the floor.

### 3. Follow-along studio lab — 12 minutes

**Setup:** Wear stable footwear or practice barefoot on a clean, nonslip surface. Clear an arm's-length space. A wall or securely fixed support is optional; do not use a rolling chair, treadmill, or unstable furniture. If balancing is difficult, keep one hand lightly on a fixed support. Stay in a comfortable range.

**Minute 0–2 — Find your base.** Stand with feet comfortably apart. Notice pressure under the heel and forefoot of each foot. Shift a small amount of weight right, then left. Keep breathing. Ask yourself: *Did I change my hip angle, my whole-body position, or both?*

**Minute 2–5 — Isolate a small knee lift.** On counts **1–2**, lift the right knee only a few inches; on **3–4**, pause; on **5–6**, lower slowly; on **7–8**, stand comfortably. Repeat three times, then change sides. Keep the range small enough to avoid gripping, pain, or loss of balance. Observe whether your pelvis tips or trunk leans. If it does, reduce the height and try again; a small adjustment is not a failure.

**Minute 5–7 — Compare two movements.** Perform the same knee lift once with a deliberately quiet torso and once with a gentle intentional trunk lean. Notice that the visible shape changes even when the basic action remains a knee lift. You are observing a difference, not deciding that one version is universally superior.

**Minute 7–10 — Original Undercurrent phrase.** Use a comfortable slow tempo, approximately one spoken count per second:
1. **1:** Step slightly right.
2. **2:** Settle weight onto the right foot.
3. **3:** Draw the left foot inward while gathering both arms.
4. **4:** Complete the gather and pause.
5. **5:** Reach the right arm diagonally upward.
6. **6:** Let the upper torso follow slightly, without forcing a twist.
7. **7:** Return the arm and torso toward center.
8. **8:** Hold a controlled, comfortable finish.

Repeat facing forward, then on a diagonal. Keep the foot placements small. If a movement is uncomfortable, mark it with your hands while seated.

**Minute 10–12 — Describe.** Write down (a) which foot carried your weight on count 2, (b) what moved on count 6, and (c) whether the torso change altered the appearance of your reach.

**Stop or modify** if you experience pain, dizziness, numbness, or repeated loss of control. Do not attempt a forced turnout or maximal leg extension to pass this lesson.

### 4. Troubleshooting without a teacher

| What you notice | Possible explanation | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| Your knee lift makes your trunk tip backward | The movement may be larger than you can comfortably control in this setup | Reduce the height by half; use a stable support; slow the lowering |
| You cannot remember the eight counts | The phrase has more components than you can retrieve at once | Practice counts 1–4 until repeatable, then 5–8, then join |
| Your standing foot shifts unexpectedly | You may be changing weight or balance strategy | Start with both feet grounded and practice only the arm reach |
| Your turnout feels strained | You may be trying to match an external visual target | Return to parallel or a comfortable natural stance; do not force the feet |

**A useful success criterion:** you can describe which leg supports your weight, reproduce the phrase twice at the same slow tempo, and explain one difference between a visible shape and joint-specific motion. This is a learning checkpoint, **not a medical safety certification**.

### 5. Check your understanding — answers included

**Question 1.** A dancer raises their knee while leaning backward. What can you infer from the visible knee height alone?

A. The dancer has unusually large isolated hip flexion.  
B. The dancer's hip angle, pelvic position, and trunk position may all contribute.  
C. The dancer has injured the hip.  
D. The knee height proves a superior technique.

**Correct: B.** A visible line can result from several body segments acting together. You would need clearer reference points or motion analysis to isolate hip movement [2, 3].

**Question 2.** Which movement best demonstrates hip abduction?

A. A small side leg lift.  
B. A forward knee lift.  
C. Bending the elbow.  
D. Rising onto the balls of the feet.

**Correct: A.** Abduction moves a segment away from the body's midline. The forward knee lift is primarily a hip-flexion example.

**Question 3.** Why should beginners avoid treating 180-degree turnout as a universal requirement?

A. Turnout comes only from the ankles.  
B. Every dancer has identical hip anatomy.  
C. Hip anatomy and available motion vary between people.  
D. Turnout has no relationship to the hips.

**Correct: C.** The IADMS anatomy resource documents structural variation affecting turnout [1].

**Question 4.** Your phrase looks different when you change facing, although you keep the same counts. Which statement is strongest?

A. The phrase is now automatically incorrect.  
B. The audience's view can change without every joint action changing.  
C. Facing determines injury risk by itself.  
D. Musical timing must also change.

**Correct: B.** Facing is a spatial variable; changing it need not change the underlying sequence.

**Scoring:** Four correct answers show understanding of these introductory concepts. If you miss one, read its explanation and repeat the corresponding movement demonstration. The questions are instructor-authored and have **not** undergone psychometric validation.

### 6. Assignment and worked example

**Your assignment:** Write 250–350 words comparing two versions of the Undercurrent phrase. Identify the reference segment for one joint action, describe the change in facing or torso position, and explain one limit of what you can infer from observation. Include a citation to [1], [2], or [3] where it supports your claim.

**Example of an acceptable analytical sentence:** “On count 6, I reached diagonally while my trunk rotated slightly. The arm appeared higher from the front, but that visual difference alone does not tell me how much movement occurred at the shoulder joint.”

**Rubric:** Terminology (25%), accurate description (25%), reasoning and limitations (25%), application and source use (25%). This is an academic rubric. It does not certify physical technique.
