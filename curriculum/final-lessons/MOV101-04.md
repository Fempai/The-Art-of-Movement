# MOV101 · Lesson 04 — Hip Mechanics, Turnout, and Safe Usable Range

**Learner-facing self-study lesson.** Approximate session: 35–50 minutes including practice, retrieval, and writing. Requires no ballet barre. Source links and metadata are included, but live browser and authenticated learner testing remain outstanding.

## Learning outcomes

After completing this lesson, you should be able to differentiate hip rotation from total visible turnout; distinguish active and passive range; create a safe 32-count synthesis phrase.

## Read and understand

The hip joint is formed by the femoral head and the acetabulum. It permits flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, and rotation, but the available range differs between dancers. Turnout is the outward orientation of the lower limb used in many ballet techniques. The visible angle of the feet can include movement from the hip and other segments, so it is not a direct measure of hip external rotation. The IADMS turnout resource explains anatomical factors that affect available motion. Scholarly reviews also show that turnout measurement protocols vary, which limits comparisons across studies. A useful range is one you can control for the specific task; a large passive stretch does not by itself demonstrate that you can lift and sustain a limb there without assistance. Do not force the feet into an angle or assume that a photograph proves current capacity.

**A useful comparison:** A door can swing only within the range permitted by its hinges and surrounding frame. A human hip is much more complex, but the comparison reminds us that individual structure matters. More force is not a solution to every limit.

**Think critically:** A visible pose is evidence of an appearance, not automatic evidence of strength, anatomical structure, safety, or artistic intention. Compare what can be observed with what would require a second camera angle, an explicit movement score, a qualified physical assessment, or a scholarly source.

## Practice with no teacher or barre

**Preparation (3–5 minutes):** Clear a nonslip space, walk or march gently, and make small comfortable movements through the ankles, hips, and shoulders. Do not use a moving treadmill or rolling chair as support. A wall or securely fixed structure can provide light support. If a movement produces pain, dizziness, numbness, or loss of control, stop or choose a seated marking alternative.

**Guided exercise (8–12 minutes):** Begin in comfortable parallel or a small natural turnout. With fixed support if needed, perform four gentle knee bends while allowing the knees to track comfortably with the feet. Return to standing. On counts 1–2, shift weight to the left; 3–4, raise the right knee a few inches; 5–6, gently straighten the right knee a small amount if comfortable while keeping the thigh nearly in place; 7–8, bend the knee and return the foot to the floor. **Anatomy check:** straightening the lower leg at the knee is *knee extension*, not hip extension or additional hip turnout. The earlier knee lift involved hip flexion. Keep the movements distinct when describing your joint actions. Repeat on the other side. Do not attempt high extensions, splits, or maximal turnout.

**Original choreography application (5–8 minutes):** For the Undercurrent synthesis, use four eight-count phrases: A (original step–gather–reach–recover), B (repeat with a small change of facing), A-prime (repeat with a slower gather), and C (return to center with a quiet ending). Mark every weight transfer and where the pelvis changes orientation.

**Independent comprehension check:** Before moving on, identify the hip action during the knee lift and the knee action during lower-leg straightening. Explain why neither action proves a particular turnout angle. If you cannot distinguish the joints, repeat the exercise in a smaller range and consult the definitions in Lessons 01 and 04.

**Repeatability test:** Perform the exercise twice with the same counts. Say which foot supports you, what segment initiates the movement, and one deliberate change between repetitions. If you cannot repeat it comfortably, reduce range and speed before trying again.

## Troubleshooting

| Observation | Possible explanation, not a diagnosis | Self-teaching adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Your knees feel pulled toward an uncomfortable angle | You may be trying to imitate a foot position that does not suit your current range | Reduce turnout or use parallel; never twist the feet against the floor |
| Your lower back arches during a small leg extension | The pelvis and trunk may be substituting for hip motion | Reduce height and return to a comfortable position |
| You feel stretching pain or pinching | The chosen range may not be tolerable | Stop the movement and avoid forcing through symptoms; seek qualified assessment for persistent pain |

## Knowledge check — attempt before reading answers

**1. Why does visible foot angle not equal isolated hip turnout?**

A. Feet cannot rotate

B. The whole lower limb and other segments can contribute

C. Hip anatomy is identical in all dancers

D. The pelvis never moves

**2. Which statement about passive and active range is best?**

A. They are always equal

B. A passive stretch proves active control

C. They describe different tasks and can differ

D. Active range is never important

**3. What is an appropriate beginner turnout target?**

A. The largest angle visible in a professional photograph

B. A comfortable position with controllable alignment

C. Exactly 180 degrees for everyone

D. An angle achieved by twisting the feet against the floor

**4. Which is the strongest conclusion from a single photo of a high leg extension?**

A. The dancer has no injury risk

B. The dancer's current passive and active ranges are equal

C. The photo shows a visible pose but cannot isolate all contributing joint actions

D. The dancer should train everyone else


### Answer key and explanations

**1. B.** Total visible turnout can reflect more than hip external rotation; the measurement method matters.

**2. C.** External assistance can place a limb where it cannot necessarily be lifted or held independently.

**3. B.** Individual anatomy varies; choose a controlled, comfortable range rather than a universal visual target.

**4. C.** A static image does not isolate hip motion or establish safety, strength, or repeatability.

**Scoring:** Four correct means you can explain these introductory ideas. If you miss a question, reread its explanation, repeat the corresponding demonstration, and answer again without looking. These are instructor-authored checks, not psychometrically validated exams or certification of physical technique.

## Assignment with example and rubric

**Submit:** (1) a count-by-count eight-count movement score; (2) 250–350 words explaining the lesson's central concept; (3) one observation versus inference comparison; and (4) one source-grounded claim with its limitation. For Lesson 04, additionally provide the 32-count A–B–A′–C synthesis.

**Worked example of analytical writing:** “I changed the timing of the gather but kept the same endpoint. The new version occupied four counts instead of two. This is an observable change in duration; it does not, by itself, prove that my muscles became stronger.”

**Rubric:** Concept accuracy 25%; movement description and count precision 25%; reasoning and limits 25%; source integration and revision 25%. Grade the written submission separately from movement quality. A website cannot reliably determine whether a movement is medically safe from a self-report.
