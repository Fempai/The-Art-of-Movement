# HIS101 · Lesson 03 — Attribution: Crediting Creators Without Inventing Origins

**Independent dance-history seminar | 45–60 minutes.** Learner-facing revision; source metadata, passage-level citation checks, and live browser testing remain pending. This course studies cultural and historical evidence; it does not assess bodies or cultural authenticity.

## Learning outcomes

Define the lesson's research concept; distinguish an observation from a historical inference; analyze how a source supports a claim; and produce a written artifact another reader can audit.

## Core lecture

Attribution means identifying the creator, performer, community, institution, or source to whom a specific claim or work is credited, with enough precision to avoid misrepresentation. Attribution and origin are different: a recorded performer may popularize a step without having invented it; a choreographer may arrange existing social movements into a new stage work. Credit should reflect what evidence actually establishes. 'Performed by,' 'choreographed by,' 'documented in,' 'associated with,' and 'created by' are not interchangeable. Copyright rules, ethical obligations, and cultural relationships are also distinct; giving credit is important but does not by itself resolve permission, representation, or ownership questions. Do not infer an individual's background or the authenticity of a dance from appearance.

**Historical reasoning principle:** The strongest claim is not the most confident-sounding claim. It is the narrowest statement the available evidence can actually support. Distinguish 'I observed,' 'this source reports,' and 'therefore I infer.'

## Guided document-analysis lab

**Step 1 — Identify the evidence (5 minutes):** Write down the creator, title, publication year, medium, and purpose of each source you examine. If a field is missing, mark it unknown rather than guessing.

**Step 2 — Apply the lesson (15–20 minutes):** Practice an attribution audit using three hypothetical captions. A: 'Performed by Dancer X in a 2018 video' (video establishes performance only). B: 'Choreographed by Dancer X' (requires a credit or reliable testimony). C: 'Invented by Dancer X' (requires stronger origin evidence). Rewrite each with the narrowest claim supported. Then make an attribution log for a real clip: title, creator/uploader, performer if identified, date, platform, quoted credit line, uncertainty, and URL. If any field is not known, write 'not established.'

**Step 3 — Challenge your own interpretation (5 minutes):** For one claim, write a plausible alternative explanation and identify what additional evidence would discriminate between the two.

**Step 4 — Connect to Undercurrent (5 minutes):** Describe the original step–gather–reach–recover motif as *your own choreography*. Do not attribute it to a historical community without evidence. Distinguish movement vocabulary inspired by your study from a documented reconstruction of a named tradition.

**Independent feedback:** Highlight every sentence asserting an origin, date, creator, or cultural generalization. Ask whether a specific source supports that exact claim. If not, narrow or remove it. Re-check quotations against originals and preserve the source URL and access date.

## Knowledge check

**1. Which claim is justified by a video showing someone performing a move?**

A. The performer created the move because they appear in the earliest video the student found

B. They performed it in that recording

C. The performer was the first to teach it because the recording includes a demonstration

D. The performer learned it from the uploader because the uploader posted the clip

**2. What does 'popularized by' mean compared with 'created by'?**

A. It means the person created the movement and then made it widely known

B. A person helped spread awareness, which does not prove invention

C. It means a creator legally transferred ownership when others adopted the movement

D. It means the earliest surviving recording proves who invented the move

**3. How should missing creator information be handled?**

A. Identify the creator from the performer's appearance in the oldest available video

B. Name the most widely credited performer as creator without checking the original attribution

C. Record that the creator is not established

D. Treat the video's upload date as the movement's creation date

**4. What does a proper citation NOT automatically grant?**

A. Verification that every claim in the cited source is historically accurate

B. Permission to reuse protected material

C. Confirmation that the credited performer is the movement's original creator

D. A guarantee that the cited recording contains no third-party rights

### Answer key with explanations

**1. B.** Performance evidence does not automatically establish invention.

**2. B.** Dissemination and invention are different claims.

**3. C.** Document uncertainty instead of inventing authorship.

**4. B.** Citation and permission are separate matters.

**Self-check:** If you miss a question, explain why one tempting wrong option overstates what the evidence proves. These original questions test conceptual distinctions, not complete historical mastery.

## Research assignment

Submit a claim-evidence table containing at least four claims with source details, limits, and revised wording. Write 250–350 words interpreting the table. For Lesson 04, submit the **600-word comparison** described above, with citations and a bibliography.

**Worked example:** “A video published in 2018 shows Performer A using a gesture. The recording documents this performance. It does not establish that Performer A invented the gesture, so my caption says 'performed by' rather than 'created by.'”

**Rubric:** Historical precision 30%; source quality and attribution 30%; explicit limitations and alternative explanations 20%; clarity and organization 20%. Do not assign a high grade merely for including many links.

## Reading and scholarly limits

**Jeff Chang (2005), *Can't Stop Won't Stop: A History of the Hip-Hop Generation*.** St. Martin's Press. https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780312425791/cantstopwontstop — historical account for hip-hop's broader social context; not evidence for every individual dance step.

**Joseph G. Schloss (2009), *Foundation: B-Boys, B-Girls and Hip-Hop Culture in New York*.** Oxford University Press. https://global.oup.com/academic/ — ethnographic account of breaking and practitioner perspectives; exact book-specific publisher URL requires verification.

**Susan Leigh Foster (1986), *Reading Dancing: Bodies and Subjects in Contemporary American Dance*.** University of California Press. https://www.ucpress.edu/ — dance scholarship on interpretation; edition and title-level link require verification.

**Jennifer Homans (2010), *Apollo's Angels: A History of Ballet*.** Random House. https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/ — a broad ballet history, with a title-specific publisher link still to be verified.

**Source caution:** These readings provide context and scholarly perspectives; the original hypothetical maps, captions, and quizzes are teaching exercises. Verify each specific historical claim against the cited passage before treating a lesson as fully source-audited.
