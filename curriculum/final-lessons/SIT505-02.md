# SIT505 · Lesson 02 — Audience Relationships: Sightlines, Distance, and Attention

**Site-Specific & Cultural Performance | graduate-level independent study | 45–65 minutes.** Authored learner-facing revision. Source verification, site-specific safety review, and browser testing remain outstanding. No public-space access or public performance is required.

## Lecture: site-responsive reasoning

**Learning goals:** Describe a site constraint, transform a movement choice in response, document access and permission limits, and distinguish verified context from artistic speculation.

A site-specific work changes with where and how people encounter it. A front-facing arrangement directs attention differently from a promenade, a circle, or a dispersed audience. Sightlines are not identical for every spectator: height, distance, obstructions, and mobility needs affect what can be seen. A choreographer can plan audience movement, but should not force people to stand, block exits, or enter inaccessible routes. This lesson studies hypothetical viewing positions rather than recruiting an audience. A map predicts possible visibility; it does not prove what actual viewers perceive.

## Guided site-design laboratory

**Prepare:** Use only a private or explicitly permitted, level, unobstructed practice area. Never block exits, rehearse in traffic, or test uninspected elevated surfaces.

**Investigate and choreograph (20–25 minutes):** Using the same safe room map, mark three hypothetical viewer positions: front, side, and seated near the entry. Keep the performance in one fixed clear zone. Perform an eight-count reach facing front, then repeat facing the side position. From each marked position, look at the empty performance area or review a fixed-camera recording to estimate whether hands and feet would remain visible. Make a three-column sightline table: viewer position, likely visible action, possible obstruction. Design one orientation change on counts 7–8 that improves visibility without adding travel.

**Troubleshoot:** If a proposed viewer position blocks the exit, move it. If you cannot physically test a viewpoint, label the table as a prediction. Do not claim that everyone sees the same emotional story from every angle.

**Undercurrent transfer:** Write a count-level step–gather–reach–recover score with the site's relevant orientation and a safer seated or reduced-travel alternative. Document which design choices respond to the site and which remain artistic preferences.

## Knowledge check

**1. What is a sightline?**

A. A musical accent

B. A potential line of view toward the performance

C. A dance genre

D. A dancer's private thought

**2. Which statement is defensible before audience testing?**

A. All viewers will feel the same

B. The site is universally accessible

C. The side position may obscure the far arm

D. The front view guarantees attention

**3. What is a responsible audience-plan revision?**

A. Move a viewer point away from a blocked exit

B. Force everyone to stand

C. Ignore seated sightlines

D. Require an unsafe route

**4. What cannot a floor plan alone establish?**

A. Where furniture sits

B. Where an exit is marked

C. The planned facing

D. How real spectators will interpret the performance

### Correct answers and explanations

**1. B.** Sightlines concern visibility from a position.

**2. C.** It is a specific visibility hypothesis.

**3. A.** Safety and access take priority.

**4. D.** Reception requires separate evidence.

**Feedback cycle:** For each missed question, distinguish a verified condition, a design hypothesis, and an unsupported assumption. These are original concept checks, not a formal accessibility or permitting assessment.

## Independent assignment

Submit a labeled site sketch, a movement score, a constraints-and-decisions table, and a 250–350-word reflection. Identify at least two uncertainties and how you would verify them. **Lesson 04 capstone:** a **site-specific proposal** including artistic rationale, 32-count score, site and audience map, accessibility and safety plan, permission status, cultural-context source ledger, and contingency plan.

**Worked example:** “The room's side window creates glare toward the front-facing performer. I rotated the reach toward the interior wall on counts 5–6 and kept the gather unchanged. This is a design response to observed light, not proof that every spectator will prefer it.”

**Rubric:** Site-responsive movement design 25%; safe and legible spatial plan 25%; accessibility and permissions transparency 30%; cultural-context research and reflective justification 20%. No points for unverified claims of legal compliance.
