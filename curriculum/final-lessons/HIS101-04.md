# HIS101 · Lesson 04 — Critical Reading: Compare Sources and Write a Defensible Dance History

**Independent dance-history seminar | 45–60 minutes.** Learner-facing revision; source metadata, passage-level citation checks, and live browser testing remain pending. This course studies cultural and historical evidence; it does not assess bodies or cultural authenticity.

## Learning outcomes

Define the lesson's research concept; distinguish an observation from a historical inference; analyze how a source supports a claim; and produce a written artifact another reader can audit.

## Core lecture

Critical reading asks what a source claims, what evidence it presents, whose perspective it centers, and what alternative interpretations remain. A scholarly monograph, practitioner interview, museum description, and promotional video serve different purposes. Publication by a reputable institution is useful context, not proof that every sentence is beyond challenge. Compare claims at the same level of specificity: a book about rap music cannot by itself verify the creator of a particular dance step; a ballet manual can document codified terminology but not every historical use of that movement. Historians also distinguish primary sources, such as contemporary records or participant testimony, from later secondary interpretations. A primary source is not necessarily accurate on every point, and a secondary source may synthesize multiple records. Your objective is a reasoned comparison with explicit limits, not a winner-takes-all ranking of traditions.

**Historical reasoning principle:** The strongest claim is not the most confident-sounding claim. It is the narrowest statement the available evidence can actually support. Distinguish 'I observed,' 'this source reports,' and 'therefore I infer.'

## Guided document-analysis lab

**Step 1 — Identify the evidence (5 minutes):** Write down the creator, title, publication year, medium, and purpose of each source you examine. If a field is missing, mark it unknown rather than guessing.

**Step 2 — Apply the lesson (15–20 minutes):** Select two distinct dance practices, such as ballet technique in a documented theatrical institution and a documented hip-hop-related social practice. Use at least two credible sources per practice (**four distinct sources minimum**), including one practitioner account or historical record per practice where available. If no suitable primary source is accessible, state that limitation and do not manufacture testimony. Make a claim-evidence table with **at least four rows, including at least two claims about each practice**, and columns: exact claim, precise source location (page/passage/timestamp), evidence type, potential limitation or disagreement, and revised defensible wording. A generic publisher homepage is not passage-level evidence. Draft a 600-word comparison with sections on context, transmission, attribution, and what remains uncertain. Avoid claims of cultural universality or single inventors without evidence.

**Step 3 — Challenge your own interpretation (5 minutes):** For one claim, write a plausible alternative explanation and identify what additional evidence would discriminate between the two.

**Step 4 — Connect to Undercurrent (5 minutes):** Describe the original step–gather–reach–recover motif as *your own choreography*. Do not attribute it to a historical community without evidence. Distinguish movement vocabulary inspired by your study from a documented reconstruction of a named tradition.

**Independent feedback:** Highlight every sentence asserting an origin, date, creator, or cultural generalization. Ask whether a specific source supports that exact claim. If not, narrow or remove it. Re-check quotations against originals and preserve the source URL and access date.

## Knowledge check

**1. Which source is best suited to document a practitioner's own account?**

A. A direct interview with that practitioner

B. A curator's summary of the practitioner's account without access to the original interview

C. A later scholar's reconstruction of the practitioner's motives without firsthand testimony

D. A performance photograph used to infer what the practitioner said privately

**2. What makes a historical comparison stronger?**

A. Using a shared movement shape as proof of an identical cultural origin

B. Stating claims at the specificity the evidence supports

C. Generalizing from one source to every performer in both traditions

D. Selecting the most convenient chronology while omitting conflicting records

**3. What should a student do when sources disagree?**

A. Cite only the earlier source and omit the conflicting later testimony

B. Choose the claim repeated most often online without evaluating its evidence

C. Describe the disagreement and evaluate the evidence

D. Average the conflicting dates and present the midpoint as a documented event

**4. Which is a valid distinction?**

A. A firsthand account cannot be mistaken about an event the speaker witnessed

B. A secondary source can synthesize and interpret multiple primary sources

C. A museum label is always primary evidence for the event it describes

D. A later secondary account automatically overrides an earlier firsthand record

### Answer key with explanations

**1. A.** A direct interview is primary evidence of what the practitioner says, though historical claims may require corroboration. B is a curator's summary, C a later reconstruction, and D an inference from an image rather than testimony.

**2. B.** Historical comparisons should make claims no broader than the cited evidence. A treats shared shape as proof of shared origin, C generalizes from one case to all performers, and D suppresses conflicting chronology.

**3. C.** Describe conflicting accounts and evaluate their provenance, timing, and evidentiary support. A hides a disagreement, B substitutes repetition for evaluation, and D invents a midpoint date that neither source documented.

**4. B.** A secondary source can compare and interpret several primary records while remaining subject to critique. A assumes eyewitness infallibility, C incorrectly classifies every museum label as primary evidence, and D assumes a later secondary account always overrides firsthand evidence.

**Self-check:** If you miss a question, explain why one tempting wrong option overstates what the evidence proves. These original questions test conceptual distinctions, not complete historical mastery.

## Research assignment

Submit a claim-evidence table containing at least four claims with source details, limits, and revised wording. For Lesson 04, **replace** the usual 250–350-word table interpretation with the **600-word comparison** of two dance practices described in the guided lab, using at least two credible sources per practice, the claim-evidence table, in-text citations, and a bibliography. Address context, transmission, attribution, and unresolved uncertainty. Include **at least two claim rows per practice**; distinguish the four distinct sources from any additional background readings. If two sources conflict, quote or paraphrase their competing claims with precise locations and explain which aspects remain unresolved.

**Worked example (structure only, not a sourced historical conclusion):** “My 600-word comparison separates context, transmission, and attribution for Practices A and B. For each, I cite two independently evaluated sources and place four narrowly worded claims in a claim-evidence table. Where a retrospective account and an institutional archive differ, I explain the disagreement and the limits of both rather than declaring either automatically authoritative. My bibliography permits readers to audit each claim.”

**Rubric:** Historical precision 30%; source quality and attribution 30%; explicit limitations and alternative explanations 20%; clarity and organization 20%. Do not assign a high grade merely for including many links.

## Reading and scholarly limits

**Jeff Chang (2005), *Can't Stop Won't Stop: A History of the Hip-Hop Generation*.** St. Martin's Press. https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780312425791/cantstopwontstop — historical account for hip-hop's broader social context; not evidence for every individual dance step.

**Joseph G. Schloss (2009), *Foundation: B-Boys, B-Girls and Hip-Hop Culture in New York*.** Oxford University Press. https://global.oup.com/academic/ — ethnographic account of breaking and practitioner perspectives; exact book-specific publisher URL requires verification.

**Susan Leigh Foster (1986), *Reading Dancing: Bodies and Subjects in Contemporary American Dance*.** University of California Press. https://www.ucpress.edu/ — dance scholarship on interpretation; edition and title-level link require verification.

**Jennifer Homans (2010), *Apollo's Angels: A History of Ballet*.** Random House. https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/ — a broad ballet history, with a title-specific publisher link still to be verified.

**Source caution:** These readings provide context and scholarly perspectives; the original hypothetical maps, captions, and quizzes are teaching exercises. Verify each specific historical claim against the cited passage before treating a lesson as fully source-audited.
