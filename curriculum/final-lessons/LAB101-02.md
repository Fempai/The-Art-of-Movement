# LAB101 · Lesson 02 — Emotional Intention Through Timing and Focus

**Original repertory | 40–55 minutes | independent learner.** This is an authored lesson revision, not a certified professional performance evaluation. Source auditing and browser testing remain pending.

## Objective and explanation

Perform two expressive versions of the same eight-count motif, changing reach timing and gaze while maintaining stable support; compare observable differences with intended meaning without claiming a universal audience interpretation.

Expressive intention can guide choices of gaze, duration, energy, and movement pathway. But an audience's emotional interpretation cannot be guaranteed. To study this distinction, perform the same motif with two different intentions and document only what changes physically. For instance, a longer reach and delayed return might support an intention of hesitation, while a quick reach and direct gaze might support resolve. These are artistic hypotheses rather than universal emotion-to-gesture translations. Avoid deliberately inducing distress or forcing emotional recall to perform a phrase.

## Follow-along studio

**Prepare (5 minutes):** Clear a nonslip area. Warm up with gentle weight shifts, comfortable shoulder circles, and small arm reaches. Keep the movement within your own pain-free range. Use seated marking when balance or standing is uncomfortable; do not attempt falls, inversions, or unsupported acrobatics.

**Guided rehearsal (15 minutes):** Begin with the Unit 1 motif. Version A: count 1 step right, 2 settle into balanced support, 3–4 gather hands, 5–6 reach slowly, 7 return the arm toward center while the gaze follows it, and 8 pause with gaze toward the hand now at center. Version B: keep the same footwork and arm pathway, but on 5 reach promptly, 6 hold, 7 return the arm, and 8 finish with gaze toward front. Perform A twice and B twice. **Seated score for both versions:** Sit in a stable nonrolling chair with feet supported; 1 shift sitting pressure slightly right, 2 settle, 3–4 gather hands. **A:** 5–6 extend the right arm slowly, 7 return the arm while gaze follows within a comfortable range, 8 pause with gaze toward the hand at center. **B:** 5 extend the right arm promptly, 6 hold, 7 return, 8 gaze forward. Keep the same supported seat and hand pathway in both versions; seated marking does not demonstrate the standing step. Film from a fixed angle if convenient, or write down which counts changed. Keep the gaze level comfortable; do not turn the neck sharply.

**Solo feedback (5 minutes):** If both versions look identical, isolate one variable such as reach speed or gaze and exaggerate only within a comfortable range. If you lose balance while looking away, keep the head neutral and change arm timing instead. Describe 'arm arrived on count 5' rather than 'the dancer definitely felt confident.'

**Memory and expressive check:** First perform while reading the score, then from memory, then compare with the score. Write two factual observations (for example, 'I reached on count 6 rather than 5') and one artistic intention (for example, 'I wanted the delay to suggest anticipation'). Do not confuse the two.

## Knowledge check

**1. Which is directly observable in a recording?**

A. The specific feeling the dancer intended the reach to express

B. A reach held through count six

C. The emotional response of a viewer who was not recorded

D. The reason the dancer chose that reach duration

**2. What changes between the two taught versions?**

A. The movement's historical origin, inferred from the different gaze direction

B. The number of supporting feet, even though both versions use the same base

C. Reach timing and gaze

D. The underlying motif's identity, even though its step–gather–reach–recover structure remains

**3. What is a responsible expressive claim?**

A. A delayed reach may support an intention of hesitation

B. A delayed reach proves that every viewer experienced hesitation

C. A gaze change establishes the dancer's private emotional state

D. A repeated expressive choice guarantees one interpretation for all viewers

**4. What should you do if gaze changes cause instability?**

A. Keep the same unstable head turn but shorten only the musical phrase

B. Repeat the same gaze change faster while maintaining the same stance

C. Keep the head neutral and change arm timing

D. Hold the head turn for longer while reducing only the arm reach

### Answer key with reasoning

**1. B.** A synchronized recording can show a reach continuing through count six. A and D infer the dancer's private intention or reason, and C invents an unrecorded viewer's response.

**2. C.** The two taught versions vary reach timing and gaze while retaining the step–gather–reach–recover motif. A invents a new historical origin, B changes support despite the stated common base, and D mistakes a variation for a different motif.

**3. A.** Saying a delayed reach *may* support an intention of hesitation appropriately distinguishes artistic interpretation from measured audience response. B and D assert universal reactions, while C treats a gaze change as direct evidence of private emotion.

**4. C.** Keeping the head neutral while changing arm timing retains an expressive contrast without the destabilizing gaze change. A, B, and D preserve or intensify the problematic head turn rather than reducing the balance demand.

**Self-correction:** Explain any missed answer in your own words and repeat the associated movement chunk at a slower count. These questions check conceptual understanding, not externally validated technique.

## Repertory assignment

Submit a written count sheet and, if you choose, a private practice recording. Document starting and ending facing, supporting foot or seated support, the movement on each count, and one deliberate revision. For Lesson 02, submit separate count sheets for versions A and B, identifying reach duration, gaze direction, stable support, one intended expressive difference, and what cannot be inferred from the movements alone.

**Worked example:** “In A, the reach traveled through counts 5–6, my arm returned on 7, and my gaze remained on the hand at center on 8. In B, the arm arrived on 5, held on 6, and my gaze returned front by 8. Those are observable differences; describing A as hesitant and B as resolved is my intention, not a guaranteed audience reading.”

**Rubric:** Two accurate and distinguishable eight-count A/B versions 30%; stable support and comfortable gaze/arm pathways in both versions 25%; separate count sheets identifying reach timing and gaze changes 25%; documented revision and clear distinction between intended expressive meaning and observable movement 20%. Self-scoring does not certify readiness for advanced physical skills.
